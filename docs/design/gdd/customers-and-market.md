# Customers and market

Customers come from nine profiles on a 3×3 grid of budget and technical skill. Each profile weighs three scores on every host, and its budget sets how much it offers to pay. This borrows King of Retail's model, where every product has quality, fashion and luxury scores and customers sit on a wealth × fashion grid ([customer wiki](https://king-of-retail.fandom.com/wiki/Customers)).

**The three scores**

| Score | Raised by | Matters most to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Performance | Hardware, resources, network capacity, latency | High technical skill |
| Reliability | HA clusters, uptime record, SLA, support response time | High budget |
| Convenience | Control panel, add-ons, one provider for everything | Low technical skill |

Low-budget customers offer the least and ask for the lowest SLA.

**The nine profiles**

| Budget \\ Skill | Low skill | Medium skill | High skill |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Low budget | Private individual: a blog, family photos | Gamer: game server and a small site | Hobby developer: the cheapest VPS |
| Medium budget | Sole proprietor: a flower shop's website | Small business: site, email, domains | Agency or startup: VPS, many sites |
| High budget | Doctor's or law office: managed, must not fail | Online shop: HA hosting, SSL, backups | Enterprise: dedicated, cloud, colocation |

**Hidden profiles.** Behind each profile the game keeps: products wanted and how likely they are bought together, budget, request size, outage tolerance, ticket habits, and requirements on the host (see Company level and reputation).

| Profile | Usually buys together |
| --- | --- |
| Gamer | Game server, small website, domain; never enterprise support |
| Sole proprietor | Web hosting, domain, email |
| Online shop | Web hosting, SSL, backups, DDoS protection |
| Enterprise | Dedicated or cloud servers, SSL (with every domain), priority support |

**Cross-selling.** Unlockable boosts raise those odds, such as a free SSL certificate with every domain or a game server and website bundle. Each gives customers a bonus and you more sales per customer.

**Market research.** Requirements start hidden. The Market Research app reveals, per profile, budget ranges, which scores they weigh, and what they demand from a host. It also shows demand by product and city, and reports on lost sales, for example "chose another host: no domains on offer".

**Competitors.** A few big, established hosts with parody names dominate the market, such as a Hetzner stand-in, an IONOS stand-in and an OVH stand-in, alongside specialists: game server hosts, VPS-only budget hosts and managed WordPress hosts. Each has its own product line with prices, and each targets different segments, so a cheap VPS host competes for hobbyists and a game server host for gamers. They run passively in the background: the game doesn't simulate their customers one by one, only their offers and their share of each segment. They are a fixed backdrop and don't react to you, but the market is crowded and hard-fought. A customer first drops hosts that miss its requirements, then compares the remaining hosts on the three scores.

**In the game.** The competitors are Ionose, OVHeat, Hetzler (also the colocation provider, whose website lists its hosting products at this year's market price times its price factor) and Rackspice, plus the specialists Nitradoh for game servers and WP Engyne for managed WordPress. Requests arrive in Support as emails: a card with the need, the requirements with a check or a cross, the target cluster's resource bars, the customer's thread and a price field with the market reference beside it. Requests you cannot serve stay in a Cannot serve box, and every request that got away is listed under Lost sales with its reason. The card does not name the customer's profile. The Customers app changes the price of a running service next to its market reference. Numbers are in economy/sales-and-quotes.md, Sales and quotes.

**Market app.** The Market research skill opens the Market app with four sections. Demand shows, per city, the buyers a month for each product, the requests that reach you and how the rest split over the named competitors. Profiles lists the nine profiles with their share of buyers; each stays locked until you buy a market study by Gartnah for it, which reveals the budget range, the size and SLA they ask for, the weights of the three scores, their price sensitivity, the products they buy and what they demand from a host. Lost sales groups the reasons of the last 3, 6 or 12 months and counts them. Competitors shows each competitor's scores, price position, product line and the profiles it sells to. Study prices are in economy/README.md, Market research.

**Undercutting and awareness.** You can price just below a competitor's offer to win more of its segment, but customers don't switch overnight. How many people know your company limits how many consider you at all, and that awareness grows slowly through marketing, reviews and word of mouth. A price cut brings a gradual trickle of customers over months, not an instant wave, and a well-known competitor keeps most of its customers even when you are cheaper.

**Domains.** Every web hosting customer comes with a domain, made once when the order or request arrives and kept on the service for good. The service is listed under that domain everywhere: order emails and requests, ticket subjects and texts, notices, the customer page (whose contact address uses it) and the product and machine lists. The rules:

- The country of the customer's city sets the top-level domain: Frankfurt, Berlin, Düsseldorf, Munich, Nuremberg and Falkenstein .de, Vienna .at, Zurich .ch, London .co.uk, Paris .fr, Amsterdam and Kerkrade .nl, Milan .it, and the American cities .com. Its language sets the words. Customers in American cities also have American names and companies (Inc instead of AG).
- A private customer uses their name (katrin-maurer.de, motto.fr), a hobby developer their name with a developer touch (frankcodes.fr, haas-dev.it), and a gamer a handle (frostottoplays.co.uk).
- A sole proprietor or office puts the trade before the surname (baeckerei-weber.de, zahnarzt-lorenz.ch, solicitors-pohl.co.uk); a small business puts the surname first (roth-logistica.it). An agency uses its brand (pixelwerk.fr), a shop its shop name, an enterprise its name with a group word (kaiser-group.co.uk).
- Names are lowercase ASCII, umlauts written out (ä is ae, ß is ss) and accents dropped. A domain is unique in the game: the next wording is tried, then a number is appended.
- The seed picks the wording, so a seed always gives the same domains. Saves from before domains get them on load, and the service takes the domain as its name.

The company's own domain comes from its name and the country of its first location (basement-hosting.de); replies use it for the mail server, the name servers, the control panel and the signature.

**Custom orders.** Every order email asks for its own resources: web hosting names its disk space, monthly traffic, mailboxes and its domain (one the customer wants registered, or one they own already, about one in three); a VPS or game server names its vCPUs, RAM and disk. Each order starts from the size its profile's budget picks, and web hosting, VPS and game server orders scale it by a factor from a half to one and a half, so no two read alike; its reference price follows the resources (see Per-resource pricing). The order ticket is worked in steps, each a button in Support and a line in the customer's work log; the customer gets four emails in all, the quote, the confirmation, the notice that the service is live and the access data. The first step is the quote; the account, the confirmation, the domain, the setup and the access data open only after the customer accepts it:

1. **Quote** is a document written in Books: Create quote picks the order and prefills the monthly price at the market's rate (or the customer's counter-offer) and the market's setup fee, with the market's beside them, on an A4 page with the setup fee, first month and domain lines. You drag the quote row onto the order ticket's attachment strip in Support, which sends it to the customer. The ticket then waits on the customer, who answers within their reply time the way a sales lead does: they take it, counter once with a price of their own, or turn it down by their budget, and the quote in Books shows the answer as accepted, countered or declined. A counter or a refusal puts the ticket back in the inbox for another quote. A customer who turns the quote down for good closes the ticket, and no account was ever made.
2. **Create customer account** opens Books at New customer with the order email pinned beside the form. The email is signed with the customer's postal address; a click on a line of it fills the focused field, the postcode line fills postcode and city, and the focus moves on to the next empty field. Save makes the customer record with that address, with no service yet, and sends no email; there is no account without an address.
3. **Confirm order** is the first invoice sent from Books. Carry forward to invoice on the accepted quote makes it as a draft at the accepted price, and the quote then reads Invoiced with the invoice number; a shop order has no quote, and Open invoice on its ticket makes the draft instead. The invoice holds the setup fee, the first month, plus the domain fee for a new domain or the transfer fee for an owned one. Sending the draft as PDF mails the confirmation. The ticket then waits on the customer, who pays by bank transfer by their usual habit (on time, late or never); the order counts as paid once you match the transfer to the invoice in Books, and the invoice status shows on the ticket.

With the Lexwear module Helpdesk link the ticket has a Create quote button that opens the quote in Books, the quote has a Send button that attaches it to the ticket, and an accepted quote creates the account from the order email and sends the first invoice by itself.
4. **Register domain** or **Transfer domain**, for web hosting, books the registry's yearly cost; the registry answers two minutes later, and the live email tells the customer.
5. **Set up** sets the service up with its custom resources and mails the customer that it is live, with the domain registered or transferred, the panel address and user name for a site or the root login for a server. You pick the host: the step lists every cluster or panel host in the order's city that sells the product, as cards with the RAM and disk booked on it against what it holds, and a Select button on each. RAM and CPU never refuse a booking; a host without the disk, without software that sells the product or with its control plane down is refused. Automatic, where the game picks the first host with room, is not offered until the company learns Provisioning automation (see Research and unlocks).
6. **Send access data** (Zugangsdaten senden) mails the login of the new service: the user name, the password and the address, which is the panel for web hosting and game servers and the server's host name with root for a VPS. Every service gets its password when it is created. A shop order has no such step, since the shop sends the access data. A customer who has no access data a day after the setup writes in with "How do I get into my account?" and loses 10 happiness; the Zugangsdaten reply on that ticket, or on any other how-to ticket of the customer, sends the data and completes the order's step.

Shop orders skip the quote: the website's prices are the quote and ordering is the acceptance. They wait in the inbox like an email order and need the account, the confirmation, the domain and the setup by hand, with the website's price on the invoice. With Provisioning automation learned, Settings has a Place orders automatically switch. With it on, a shop order is invoiced and placed through the shop's module the moment it arrives, and an email order runs the account, confirmation, domain and an Automatic setup in one go the moment the customer accepts the quote. A step that cannot run, such as a setup with no room, leaves the order there for you. The quote stays yours to send.

You may set up before the money is in and risk never being paid, or wait. Once paid, an order without its service goes back to the inbox and is due within the customer's reply time on their clock; waiting longer costs happiness like any late answer. A paid order with its service and its access data sent is done; one that is paid but still owes the access data goes back to the inbox. An order that is never paid goes through dunning as usual: collections closes the account and ends the service. Declining a confirmed order, or letting one lapse before it is confirmed, closes the account and writes off its invoice. An accepted quote that is not worked within three days lapses. The first invoice prepays the service's first 730 hours, so the monthly bill starts after them. Support staff trained on orders work the steps after the quote in order, set the order up on the first host with room, and leave the setup until the invoice is paid; the quote stays with the player.

**Per-resource pricing.** Every order, sales request, upgrade, service and lost sale is a spec: the resources the customer asks for. Web hosting asks for disk, traffic and mailboxes; a VPS, game server or cloud server for vCPUs, RAM, disk and traffic; a dedicated server for the smallest server RAM it accepts; colocation for rack units and watts; storage for gigabytes; DNS for zones and VPN for users, both with traffic; and a domain or SSL for one service. Requests, emails, Sales rows, tickets, the customer's service cards and events all write a spec the same way, for example "VPS, 2 vCPU, 4 GB, 80 GB", "Web hosting, 2 GB, 3 mailboxes" or "DNS hosting, 12 zones". Each line charges a base fee per service plus a price per unit, traffic per 100 GB included, and the market's rates per unit set every market reference (numbers in economy/README.md, Per-resource pricing).

Prices are set per shop product (`shop.md`, section 4b); every unit and setup fee otherwise costs the market's rate. The shop charges a product's price, less any campaign discount, and, with Provisioning automation switched on, places the order at once without asking the customer; without it the order waits in Support for its steps. Overpricing costs you through customer choice, where your price index brings fewer leads, and through price tolerance and churn. Services already sold keep the price they were sold at; to raise a running service you change its price in the Customers app, which goes through the usual price tolerance, complaints and retention. Email orders are quoted by hand, starting from the market reference.

Traffic is cheap on purpose: hosts in 2005 bundled it, so it is a few percent of a typical bill, more on game servers and colocation, and its rate falls as traffic grows. The market's traffic rate takes its share out of the line's other rates, so an average customer pays what they paid before traffic was priced.

**Setup fees.** Each line in the shop has a one-time setup fee in Pricing, next to its rates, with the market's fee beside it; empty charges the market's. When a shop order arrives and is placed, the customer gets an invoice at once with the setup fee and the full first month, and the monthly run starts billing the service after that prepaid month. A fee above the market's makes the shop a little less attractive to leads, and one below a little more, through the same price weighing as the rates. An email order carries a setup fee on its confirmation invoice too: the quote step has a Setup fee field next to the monthly price, prefilled with the line's fee, and the invoice lists it before the first month.

Saves from before per-resource pricing give every service, request and lost sale that named a plan that plan's size as its spec in the year it started, and start with every rate at the market's. DNS and VPN services from before zones and users get the count their price buys at the market's rates, and setup fees start at the market's.

**Email orders.** With a shop active, the website card in Marketing has a Take orders by email switch, on by default and in older saves. Turning it off removes the contact call to action from the website, and orders by email stop: every order lead uses the shop, and a shop order that cannot be placed (no room, or the product is not sold in that city) is cancelled and is a lost sale. Business customers, upgrades and the shady VPS request still arrive by email. It is a real trade: it saves the step work of a quarter of the order leads and costs the orders the shop cannot place.

**Company email.** A new company starts on a free GMix box, which needs no domain and delivers like any other service; its address is the company name at gmix.com, and the website shows it as the sales address. Moving to mail under the own domain is optional and needs the domain first. Businesses trust a freemail sales address less: while the company is on GMix, only half of the email orders from sole traders, small businesses, agencies, offices, online shops and enterprises arrive, while private users, gamers and developers write as usual. Shop orders are unaffected. The company signs up for mail on the provider websites in the Browser (the Email providers category of the index), and switching is the real walk: sign up at the new provider, create the mailboxes, move the MX record at the registrar, import the old mail on the new side and cancel the old provider, whose pages list what has not moved yet. The upgrade offers two ways. External providers charge per mailbox and month. Every hire gets a mailbox under the company domain, and a company on GMix alone, or on a plan with no mailbox left, cannot hire until it has one: Hosted Exchange by Ionose from 2005 (EUR 10 a seat, falling to 6 by 2015), Goggle Workspace from 2007 and Lookout 365 from 2011. External mail is always up and its address is never listed. Self-hosted mail is software on a server or in a VM, installed like a panel from the installer on a server's or a VM's console: Postfixit from 2005 and Mailpig from 2015, free, one mail server per company. Installing one beside a provider adds the server as a second mail host, and the MX record decides which one receives. A mail server needs a public address, so on a dynamic home line its address is always listed and only a business line makes it work from the basement. While the mail host is down or its address is listed, delivery is impaired: half of the order emails arrive and invoices sent in that window are paid 3 days later. Cancelling a provider or uninstalling the mail server returns the company to GMix. Support tickets arrive in the Support app regardless.

**Upgrades.** A service's upgrade request asks what upgrading would cost and names the resources it needs, from the current to the requested amount (one and a half to two times each). The request shows both and the price difference a month; you set the new monthly price and accept it in one step, which the customer takes, counters (accept again at or below the counter) or refuses. Taken, the service grows in place when its server has room and is otherwise placed again on the target you pick.

**Domain registration.** Each TLD has its registry: DENIQ for .de, nick.at for .at, SWISCH for .ch, Nominot for .co.uk, AFNIQUE for .fr, SIDNey for .nl and Registrato.it for .it. A domain is registered or transferred for a year at the registry's cost of that year; you renew it by hand in **Billing, Domains**, which lists every domain with its customer, registry, expiry and yearly cost, and the customer page lists the customer's own. Renewing pays the registry and invoices the customer the yearly fee. A month before expiry a notification reminds you; a domain left to expire takes its site offline, and the customer complains like about any outage until you renew it. Domains of customers who left are not renewed.

**Registrar partners.** The company resells domains through one registrar partner at a time, signed in **Network, Domains**: NameCheep (no signup, no fee, one rate for every size), GoDuddy Reseller (a monthly fee, rates falling with size), InterNetY (a signup fee and a monthly minimum of which the unused part is lost; .de at the registry's own cost in every tier) and OpenSRSly (a signup fee, rates falling with size). The partner's wholesale for a domain is the registry's cost of the year times the partner's factor for the current tier, plus the ICANN fee on a .com. The tier follows the number of domains under management (the company's and the customers'), checked at every month start: 0, 100, 500 and 5,000 domains. Billing is monthly: at a month start the partner invoices the signup once, the fee (or the part of the minimum the domains did not use) and one twelfth of the yearly wholesale of every domain under management. Registering or renewing a domain no longer books the registry cost; the customer still pays the yearly fee. Switching partners transfers every domain, a hand task of 5 minutes per domain that the player does in Domains or hands to a technician trained on panels; nothing is down during the transfer, and the new partner's signup comes with the next month start.

**The company domain.** Going online needs a domain of the company's own. Once a contract is signed, the player registers a name in Network, Domains (3 to 40 letters, digits or hyphens in front of a TLD the registries sell, not a customer's); it joins the domains under management, renews with the contract, and is the domain of the website, the sales address and the mail server. Switching a line on in Admin and setting up the web design are refused until it exists. Until then the texts that need a domain use the name the company name suggests. Saves from before registrars load with that suggested name registered at NameCheep.

**Hostnames.** Every server has a hostname, `node` and a three-digit number in purchase order (`node001`), and every VM of your own has `vm` and the next number (`vm001`). Every customer service except domains and SSL certificates has a hostname made of a product prefix (web, vps, game, dns, vpn, dedi, store, cloud, colo) and an eight-digit number. The first number lies between 10,000,000 and 19,999,999 for each game and each new service adds 1 to 97, so the numbers look like a real provider's. A customer's workload carries its service's hostname. The full name is the hostname under the company domain, for example `vps12312312.backbone.de`. Customers quote it in tickets ("vps12312312.backbone.de takes ages to answer tonight") instead of their own name for the service, and replies from support use it too. The hostname shows on the customer page (service rows and cards), in the hypervisor console (VM list and VM page) and under the label in the server inspector. A save from before hostnames numbers servers by purchase date and services by start date when it loads.

**Reach and new cities.** Only buyers in cities where you have a location can buy from you, and only a share of each city's new customers ever looks at your offer. That share grows with your level, from about 2% in the basement to about 6% for a Datacenter Baron, so marketing fills your reach in a city and new locations widen it. Once your awareness is up, opening a location in another city is the main way to keep growing; a 1/8 rack share there costs €32 to €140 a month.
