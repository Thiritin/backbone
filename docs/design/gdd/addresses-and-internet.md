# Addresses and internet

Every server reaches the internet through an address, and addresses cost money. Numbers in economy/addresses-and-internet.md, Addresses and internet.

**Internet is never free.** A new game has no line. The player orders one in Locations and pays it monthly. A consumer line has one dynamic address that the router shares with every basement machine; the provider forces a reconnect every night at 03:00, a few minutes offline that business customers notice and dislike. A business line costs more, keeps a static address and brings a /29: the router's address and four more to assign to machines, with more for a monthly fee. A dynamic line can only sell web hosting, since every other product needs an address of its own, and the web host needs a Web server share on the router, which goes to one machine per line. Business customers still buy on a dynamic line and grow unhappy with the nightly drops.

**Datacenter addresses.** In a rented location every server holds its own public IPv4 from the provider, about €1 a month at the start, cheaper per address as the company rents more, and rising after RIPE runs out of IPv4 in 2019. IPv6 arrives in datacenters in 2010 and on home lines in 2013 and costs next to nothing. Later the company can join RIPE as a LIR, get an AS number and its own blocks: a free first allocation that shrinks over the years, then /24s bought on the transfer market and announced from a datacenter.

**Customers need addresses.** Every machine takes a private address from its location's router by DHCP; the router's web interface lists them and can fix one to a machine. A VPS, game server, cloud server, VPN, dedicated server or colocation needs a public address of its own, and a sale fails without a free one. Some web hosting orders ask for a dedicated IPv4 for SSL or mail before SNI, and from 2012 a growing share asks for IPv6. The dedicated IPv4 has its own price per address in Pricing.

**Moving between locations.** Customers move between any two locations over the internet as a cold move, and "Move to" on a server ships the whole server with its workloads to a rack with room elsewhere; both cost downtime, and the server move also costs shipping and hand work at the far end. Services that change location get new IPv4 addresses from the target, so the move needs free addresses there. Customers with a dedicated address lose a little happiness because their DNS must change, less when they were told beforehand. A company that announces its own block at both locations keeps its addresses and renumbers nothing. Numbers in economy/workloads-and-migration.md, Workloads and migration.

**Reputation.** Every IPv4 has a mail reputation. Spam from a hacked panel host or a shady customer lowers it, and so do spam, phishing, attack and copyright reports in the abuse inbox. Below a threshold the address gets blocklisted and mail customers behind it write in about bounced mail. Delisting is a hand task in the address list once the spam stopped. Dynamic home line addresses are always listed.

**The address list.** Network lists each location's addresses with what holds them, their reputation and listing. The player buys and releases addresses, requests a delist, joins RIPE and announces blocks there. A server's MAC and a VM's addresses show in their inspector: private, public IPv4 (shared on a basement line) and IPv6.
