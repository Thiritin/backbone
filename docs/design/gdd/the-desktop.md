# The desktop

The whole game is a macOS-style desktop. Every system is an app in the dock that opens in its own window, and notifications slide in at the top right so you always know what is happening.

The menu bar holds the time controls, date and cash. The game saves itself in the browser. The logo menu on the far left shows your company logo; it exports the save to a file, imports one, and deletes all progress after a confirmation. A first visit, or one after deleting, opens a start screen with a New game button. Settings keeps the company name, logo mark and colour under Company, above Appearance (light, dark or automatic), Sound (a master switch and a volume slider, on at 60% by default) and Notifications (one Allow and one Sound switch per app, both on by default, and a list of notification types per app). A new game opens the Getting started guide without any popup, and the guide also sits on the desktop as an icon. The guide is the only tutorial, and every step is optional. It teaches where things are: a step carries no controls of its own, only an Open button, and it ticks once the player has done it in that app. Its steps name the place where each is done and carry an Open button that opens that app on the right view, in this order: "Choose the name, logo and colour of your company." (Settings; any saved change under Company ticks it), "Buy your first server in the Shop." (Shop), "Mount it in a rack, cable it to the router and power it on in Locations." (the basement), "Order an internet line in Locations under Internet line." (the line card), "Open the server's console, press F11 during POST, boot from USB and install a hypervisor or a web panel." (the server), "Build your first website in Marketing under Channels." (Marketing, Channels; done once the site is written), "Buy a domain for your company at NameCheep in the Browser." (the Browser, with no address; the partner's name replaces NameCheep once a registrar contract exists), "Choose where your website runs, launch it in Marketing and list your first product under Products." (Marketing, Channels; done once the site is launched and lists a product), "Choose an email provider in the Email app." (the email settings; done once the company leaves free GMix) and "Answer your first customer in Email." (Email, or Support once a desk is installed). A step that is done loses its Open button and gains a tick.

**Pear One and the Pear Store.** The desktop is Pear One, an all-in-one hosting platform from Pear, a fruit company with a familiar look. A new game starts with the starter apps in the dock: Applications, Locations, Shop, Browser, Terminal, Support (shown as Email), Billing and Settings. Customers, Marketing, Security and Research arrive later, and Staff and Market are offered, each when its problem does ([tutorial](../tutorial.md)). Getting started and Notes are desktop documents and stay out of the dock. Every other app sits in the Pear Store, the app store for Pear One. An item whose skill the company has not learned yet shows the skill it needs, and its button opens that skill in Research; once the skill is learned the item shows Get. The store never shows research costs. Installing an app adds it to the dock, and apps unlock further features as you go. Because of EU competition rules, some software comes in several competing products to choose from, such as the virtualization platform and monitoring. The introduction text lives in docs/design/intro.md.

**The store lives in the Shop.** The Pear Store is the Software class in the Shop's sidebar. It opens with a banner in the Pear style: the Pear logo and "Unlock the future today. Great upgrades to your operating system that make your life easier." Below the banner, a featured app and curated rows (Essentials, Growth, Upgrades) list each app with its icon, subscription and a Get, Open or Research button; clicking one opens its page with what it includes, its price and Install or Cancel. An app has to be researched (or reached by company level) before you can buy it, and buying it installs it in the dock. Every app in the dock shows a count of what waits for you, such as open tickets in Support, unpaid invoices in Billing or failed parts in Locations. Apps also sell upgrades as cards of their own. The desktop widgets (calendar, open tickets, cash, monthly revenue, customers and level) are upgrades too: a new game has none, and each one appears on the desktop once bought. Widgets need no research.

**Searchlight.** Searchlight is a Pear Store upgrade at 2 euro a month in 2005 that needs no skill. Once it is installed, Ctrl+K or Cmd+K opens a field in the centre of the desktop and Escape closes it; before it is owned the shortcut does nothing. Results update as you type and come in groups: Apps, Customers, Services (by hostname, label or domain), Machines (servers and your own VMs by hostname, label or address), Tickets (by subject), Sites and consoles (the company site, customer sites and the console address of every server and VM), Locations and the Settings sections. The arrow keys move through the results and Enter opens the result in its app: a customer in Customers, a server in Locations, a VM in Virtual machines, a ticket in Support, an address in the Browser. A group shows up to 6 results and the list at most 24. A match at the start of a hostname or name ranks first, then a match at the start of a word, then any other match; ties go to the newest ticket, the newest customer or the most recent purchase.

**What is new.** The save remembers which changelog entries it has been shown. Loading a save with newer entries opens a Pear OS update sheet that lists them by day, newest first, with an Open button on every entry that names an app in the dock; closing the sheet marks them seen, and a new game starts with every current entry seen. The What's new item in the logo menu opens the sheet with the whole changelog.

**Subscriptions.** Pear One is a funnel. The base is free, since you already paid for the hardware, and every app beyond the starter set is a feature expansion from the Pear Store with a monthly subscription, billed through the Bank like rent. Some expansions also sell in-app purchases: one-off extras such as an instant invoice run, alert text messages or extra dashboard slots. Prices scale with your size (per device, per customer or per employee), and Pear raises them every year, sometimes sharply, with a cheerful email about "new value". Cancelling a module removes the app from the dock and switches its features off, but keeps its data, so you can come back. Installing pays the first month at once and needs the cash for it; every later month is billed on the first with the other bills. Modules are a real cost to weigh against the work they save, and a steady drain that grows with the company.

**The Terminal.** A window of 760 by 480 on near-black metal with the prompt `you@desk $`; it is a starter app, free, and the Pear Store lists it for older saves. The text it prints imitates the real tools. From the desk: `ssh <host>`, `ping <host>`, `traceroute <host>`, `host <name>`, `help` and `clear`. A host is a server hostname (`node001`), its full name under the company domain, a private address, or for `ping`, `traceroute` and `host` also a VM or service hostname and a management address. `ssh` opens a session on a server only when it is working, not hung, and reachable over the network path from the basement desk; otherwise it prints `ssh: connect to host <name> port 22: Connection timed out`. A VM or customer service answers `Permission denied (publickey).` and a management address refuses the connection. The prompt inside a session is `root@<host>:~#` and offers `uptime`, `df -h`, `free -m`, `top` (one snapshot of the server's CPU, RAM, disk and workloads), `shutdown -h now` (the graceful shutdown), `reboot` (a graceful shutdown followed by a boot, which never corrupts data), `recover <service hostname>` and `exit`. `recover` starts the 30 minute repair of a corrupted workload, prints its progress, and reports the outcome in the window once the game clock has passed it; a web space inside a panel VM is repaired through its VM. A session ends when the server stops answering. Anything else prints `bash: <command>: command not found`, and desk commands are not available inside a session. Open sessions, the screen and the command history belong to the window and survive a reload, not a new game. A hired technician runs the same `recover` on their own while the Recover corrupted data rule is on, which it is by default.

**The Browser.** A window of 1100 by 720 in Pear style: a tab strip, back, forward, reload and a home icon, an address bar with a star for bookmarks and a bar of bookmarks. Home is the Yahoy! site index at www.yahoy.com/dir/, where a new browser session opens; it lists every site by category for the game year, from the operating systems and every software to the company's own consoles, which show as down when their host cannot be reached. Every address resolves from the game state. The company domain, with or without www, is the company site once it is launched, and not found before. Every web hosting customer's domain is that customer's site, drawn from the customer's name and profile, and defaced when its host was compromised. Machine and service names from Hostnames (`node001`, `vm001`, `vps12345678` under the company domain) and private addresses typed as URLs reach the machine and show a page that says the host serves no web page; the later consoles replace it. Every hardware manufacturer has a site at its pun under `.com` (`dwell.com`), or `.de` where the pun is German, with Home, Products, News and Support pages in two eras (software.md, 8.6). `youtub.com` shows a single video page. Any other address shows Not found. A site on a host that is down shows Cannot reach, a site on a slow host (an overloaded node, a PHP upgrade, a RAM-crowded panel host) shows a progress bar for 2 seconds first (none with reduced motion), and the rented company site is always fast. Each site kind has a 16 px code-drawn favicon: the company colour for the company site and Admin, a letter for a customer, the brand mark for a manufacturer, a play button for the video, a cross for an unreachable host. Default bookmarks (the company site once launched, and the registrar) are derived; the star adds the current address to the user's own bookmarks, kept per browser session. Tabs and bookmarks are interface state, not game state.


**Alerts.** A few events pause the game and open a sheet, like the bankruptcy warning, because the player has to look at them before time moves on. They are: cash below zero, a missed loan payment, a loan called in, every stage of a PHP security advisory (announcement, proof of concept, agency warning, patch) and a compromised panel host, lost customer data, a server seized by the authorities, the start of a DDoS attack, and a location that lost power. Later features add their own: a critical vulnerability on the player's own software, an extortion ticket after a breach, and corrupted data after a pulled plug. The sheet shows the event's title and text, up to two figures, an Open button for the app that handles the event (Bank, Security, Locations, Support or Network) and a Continue button that resumes the speed from before the pause; Escape is Continue. Opening the app leaves the game paused. Every alert also goes into the notification history, bankruptcy keeps its own sheet, and there is no setting to switch alerts off. The list lives in `ALERT_KEYS` in src/sim/alerts.ts; every other event stays a banner.

**Updates.** The build writes its id into version.json next to the page. Every five minutes and whenever the tab regains focus the page fetches that file; when the id differs from the build it runs, a sheet offers Reload and Later. Later keeps quiet for an hour. The game and open windows survive the reload. The dev server never offers one.

**Notifications.** Banners for new tickets, overdue invoices, hardware failures, attacks and staff requests. Each one opens the right app at the right place, and a notification center keeps the history. Alerts also pause the game (see below).

**Notification types.** Inside an app, notifications belong to types such as Bills and tax, Tickets, Attacks and saturation or Reviews. In Settings, under Notifications, every app row expands to its types, each with an On and an Off button, next to the app's Allow and Sound switches. A notification shows when its app is allowed and its type is on; a type that is not mentioned is on, so older settings keep every type on. Alerts show whatever the settings say. The gear in the notification center header opens Settings on the Notifications section, and the gear on a notification there expands that notification's app. The groups live in `NOTIFY_TYPES` in src/ui/notify-types.ts, and a test checks that every event the sim emits lands in exactly one group of its app; a key no group claims falls into Other.

**News.** The News app announces what becomes available, on the day it does, as a banner from the app News and as a headline in its list, newest first, each with a sentence that names a punned outlet: Heize online for hardware and software, Golem.dy for the internet and Handelsblah for the market. The announcements are a software's first year (1 January), a server generation (the day it reaches the shop), IPv6 at datacenters (2010) and on home lines (2013), RIPE's last /8 (September 2012) and run-out (November 2019), a website design generation (every four years from 2009), other gear entering the shop (one item per year), a new server type, a software feature that arrives in a later release, a help desk, a training with a first year, home lines (one item per year), a new port speed, free SSL certificates (2016), a manufacturer's releases of a month (one item per brand, with a link to the News page of its site), on 1 January the year's releases of the software families the player has installed on a cluster and of PHP when a panel runs (software new that year is left out), and, checked at each month start and told once per line, a product line the player could deliver but has not put on the website. What exists when a game starts is not announced, and a saved game does not replay what happened before it was saved. The News types in Settings (Hardware, Software, Internet, Market) can silence the banners without emptying the list. The News app is opened with the News button in the notification center and has no dock icon. The list lives in `NEWS` in src/sim/80-business/news.ts; `GameState.news` keeps what was published.

**Reloading the tab.** A reload of the page reopens the saved game with the same windows where they were, and Support, Books, Staff, Locations (with its cabling view) and the Pear Store keep their selections, tabs and filters. The clock keeps the speed it had; a new game starts paused. A closed tab starts from the desktop with no windows open.

**Challenges and leaderboard.** Every week has a challenge: the same seed for everyone, Frankfurt, and one twist. The seed comes from the ISO week (`challengeFor` in src/sim/10-catalog/challenges.ts), and the week number picks the twist from `CHALLENGES` in turn: SDSL 2048 is already in as the home line, every datacenter except Hetzler Park starts full, or the company starts with €2,000 instead of €3,500. The start screen has a Weekly challenge button next to New game, with the week, its twist and the top three by net worth. Every successful action the player takes is logged with its game hour (`s.log`, at most 50,000 entries), so a run is its seed, its challenge and that log, and anyone can replay it (`replay` in src/sim/92-run/run.ts). A run has three scores, counted until 1 January 2008: net worth at the end of 2007, the day level 3 was reached, and the mean of the hourly average customer happiness. Settings, Leaderboard shows the game's scores and exports the run file once 2007 is over; below it, the challenge's board ranks the top 50 by any of the three scores, with company name, colour and seed. Runs are submitted as a pull request that adds the file to `leaderboard/`; a GitHub workflow replays each run with the build it was made with and fails when a score differs, and after the merge it rebuilds `public/leaderboard.json` per build and challenge. A board belongs to one build, so runs from an older build stay on its board. While a challenge with runs is played, a desktop widget reads "At this date the leader had N customers and X", from the net worth leader's weekly samples.

**Sounds.** Short synthesized cues mark the events worth hearing: a chime arpeggio when a month closes (with a cash-register rattle and an extra top note when the month made money), a coin ping for paid orders and won deals, the ticket ding for everything else in Support, a soft falling two-tone for failures, outages, attacks and missed payments, a rising triad when an employee finishes training, and a bloom when the company learns a skill. Windows tick quietly as they open and close. A burst of events plays only its most important sound, each sound plays at most once every one and a half seconds, and nothing plays while the game is paused or the tab is hidden.

**Look.** Clean and light like macOS, with a dark mode. Interface text in Inter, numbers in a monospace font so columns line up, icons from Lucide. Rack and server fronts are drawn in code, so every device has a consistent, detailed front.

**Context menus.** Every object with actions has a Pear context menu: right-click it, or click its three-dot button on a touchpad. Rack devices, devices in storage, racks, ports, workloads, VMs, customers, tickets, panel hosts, invoices, domains, staff and loans all use it, so a window shows an object's state and keeps buttons for its main flow only, such as a ticket's replies or the steps of an order. The menu works with arrow keys, Enter and Escape, and submenus hold the choices of one action, such as Migrate to or Add to cluster. Destructive items (sell, fire, write off) still ask in a sheet, and an item that cannot run reports why as the usual action error. Sheets that switch between views use tabs sized to their labels, as in the server setup.
