# Support

Support is the first job that overwhelms you. Every outage, question, install request and cancellation arrives as a ticket in the Support app, next to order emails and sales requests. The app is called Email until the company buys a support desk, and the internal app id stays "Support" everywhere (saves, notification types, alerts, Searchlight, focus requests); only the shown name, dock icon and window style change.

**Email.** Without a desk the app is a plain inbox: one list of every open email, newest first, unread in bold, and a ticket view with the reply choices, resolve and the order steps. It has no kind filters, search, assignment, template picker or queue boxes. Its dock icon and window follow the company's mail service: Goggle Workspace shows a four-colour envelope in a white window with coloured labels and a rounded header, Lookout 365 a blue envelope with an O and a dark blue header bar with dense rows, Hosted Exchange by Ionose the Ionose mark in a teal-accented list, and self-hosted mail (Postfixit or Mailpig) a plain envelope called Pear Mail in a light list under a grey header. A company without mail service shows the Pear Mail envelope dimmed. Marks are code-drawn parodies.

**Orders.** An order email is a ticket with its own steps. It starts with the quote: the customer has no account until they accept a price, and a declined quote closes the ticket. After the acceptance the order is worked as account, confirmation with the invoice, domain and setup, and the setup is a pick from the hosts of the order's city with what each has booked. Provisioning automation adds Automatic to that pick and a Settings switch that runs the steps after the quote by themselves. The steps and numbers are in Custom orders (Sales) and in economy/sales-and-quotes.md, Sales and quotes. The setup preselects the best host, and every automatic placement ranks hosts the same way: hosts with disk room first, preferring one whose installed RAM covers the booking, then the newest hardware generation, then the fewest hours down this month per service it carries. The player still sets the order up; only staff, who cannot ask, and Automatic place it without that click.

**Support desks.** A hosted desk (Zendusk, Freshdusk) is subscribed to on its vendor's site in the Browser, with a tenant address under the vendor's domain, and is charged once for the owner and once for every employee on the support desk, the first month at sign-up and every month start after, with the yearly rise. A self-hosted desk (OsTickit, FreeScoot) is free: its site lists the release, and it is installed into a web space of a panel host, where it takes one account against the panel's licence and runs the newest release out that day. One desk at a time: a new desk replaces the first and drops its settings, and the tickets stay. With a desk the app is called Support, shows the desk's mark and window style, and the desk decides which features exist: the kind filters, a search field, assignment to agents, the reply template picker (without it the player picks from the first three replies), due-in timers on rows, the customer card beside the ticket, and a Reports tab with tickets per kind and per month. The inbox, sales, type and incident boxes belong to every desk. Each desk follows its real counterpart, and a feature appears in the year the real product got it. OsTickit (free and self-hosted, from 2005, grey tables) has filters, search, assignment and templates from the start, timers and reports from 2013, the customer card from 2014 and merge from 2019. Zendusk (hosted, from 2007, dark green sidebar) has everything from the start except timers, which arrive in 2013. Freshdusk (hosted, from 2011, teal header with cards) has everything from its launch. FreeScoot (free and self-hosted, from 2018, light blue list) has filters, search, assignment and the customer card, merge from 2020, and leaves templates and reports to paid modules. Only the hosted desks gain AI, in the years their counterparts shipped it; prices and dates are in economy/pear-one-subscriptions.md, Pear One subscriptions.

**Answering a ticket.** Each ticket offers a few pre-written replies and predefined actions, such as restart the service, issue a credit, escalate to a technician, or process the cancellation. The right choice closes it; a wrong one brings a follow-up. Each customer expects an answer within a set time, which depends on their segment and profile (priority support shortens it).

| Ticket type | Triggered by | Typical actions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outage report | A service the customer uses goes down | Status reply, credit, escalate |
| How-to question | New customers; cheap control panels | Template answer |
| Billing question | Invoices and payment reminders | Explain, resend invoice, grant an extension |
| Suspension appeal | A high-budget customer's services are suspended | Unsuspend with a payment extension, keep suspended |
| Expansion request | Customer nears the size they rent | Opens the sales request |
| Cancellation | Unhappy or departing customer | Retention offers, process the cancellation |
| Colocation install | Colocation customer ships hardware | Book an install with a technician |
| Abuse report | Spam or attacks from a customer's server | Warn, suspend |
| Copyright notice | A rights holder reports a URL on a customer's domain | Forward to the customer, take the content down, reject |
| Legal request | A government agency asks for data or a takedown | Comply, object, ignore at your peril |
| Refund request | An outage or a data loss hit the customer | Refund one month, credit 10%, refuse |
| Migration offer | The player offers a customer a move | None; the customer answers |

**Retention offers.** A cancellation ticket shows the options as cards in the order 50% off the next month, one month free, 20% off for 3, 6 or 12 months, free upgrade, apology and fix, and move to a better server. Each card shows what it costs, or for the upgrade the new size and for the move the target host, and the word for the odds: Unlikely, Even or Likely. The odds depend on the customer's happiness, why they are leaving (price, an outage or slowness) and their profile; the numbers are in economy/happiness-and-churn.md, Happiness and churn. A cancellation says in its email why the customer leaves. Cards the customer cannot take do not appear: a free upgrade needs room on the service's server, the fix promise needs an outage or slowness as the reason, and the move needs a host in a datacenter location that can take the service. Discounts run for the months chosen and then the price returns. A kept customer on the move option has already agreed to it, so the migration runs at their window without another answer. The apology holds the ticket for three days and then checks that the service is back and fast again; if it is not, the customer leaves. Support staff pick the cheapest option that is at least Even, and process the cancellation when there is none.

**Planned maintenance.** "Plan maintenance" on a server (right-click and inspector), a cluster or a location takes a start, offered as tonight, tomorrow night or the next weekend night at 01:00, and a length of 2, 4 or 8 hours. The sheet lists the affected services and customers and what each will answer. Planning sends every affected customer a Maintenance notice at once. Online shops that have the window on a weekday between 08:00 and 20:00 write back asking to move it, and gamers write back declining a Friday or Saturday night; the player can move the window at no cost (every customer gets a new notice) or close the ticket. Customers who accepted are covered: for those told at least 24 hours ahead an outage inside the window costs nothing, and a later announcement halves the penalties (economy/happiness-and-churn.md, Happiness and churn). Windows show on the server inspector, the cluster and the location page with their start, length and customers, and can be moved or cancelled until they start. Starting and ending a window raises a notification.

**Migration offers.** "Offer migration" on a service (the customer page, or the web spaces and VMs of a host) picks a target from the hosts the service can move to and sends the customer a Migration ticket. The player has no reply or action on it; the customer answers once their reply time is up. Below happiness 40 they decline and the ticket resolves. Otherwise they accept and name a window by their segment: players the first 02:00 to 06:00 after the answer, businesses the next weekday from 18:00 to 22:00, private customers any time. The move never runs by itself: the player makes it, by hand or with a technician, as the normal move with its downtime (economy/workloads-and-migration.md, Workloads and migration). Players and businesses get a notification when their window opens, and a window that closes without the move costs 10 happiness and reopens the ticket; private customers give 48 hours with the same result. The ticket resolves when the move is done or started. A done move counts as announced for the new addresses and gives +5 happiness when it leaves the basement for a datacenter. The player can close a reopened ticket. "Announce migration" on a host offers every service on it the same target in one step. Closing the ticket withdraws the offer.

**Unanswered tickets.** A customer whose ticket gets no answer long past its due time gives up and closes it themselves, marked Ignored. They have already lost happiness for every hour of waiting, and closing stops that loss; the limits per ticket type are in [economy/README.md](../economy/tickets-and-work-rates.md). Cancellations are processed at the next billing date instead, and notices, legal requests and orders are not the customer's to close.

**Abuse and copyright notices.** Rights holders and complainers write in about URLs on your customers' domains. Studios such as Warner Bras. or Universal Pickchers send copyright notices, and a YouTuber demands you remove a site that called their video bad and links it. Some notices are real, some are fake, and some point to domains you don't even host. Each one names the URL, so you can check it against your customers before acting. Taking down content on a fake notice angers the customer; ignoring a real one invites the next, angrier letter.

A notice comes from its sender, not the customer, and shows the sender's address and the URL. Real notices come from the sender's own domain, fake ones from a free mail address, and the YouTuber is always fake. The notice offers three actions:

- **Forward to the customer** settles a real notice, and the customer removes the content. A shady customer leaves it up, so the next notice follows anyway. Forwarding a fake one mildly annoys the customer.
- **Take content down** takes the named service offline for 48 hours and settles the notice. On a fake notice it angers the customer.
- **Reject** is the right answer to a fake notice, and the only one for a domain no customer uses. Rejecting a real notice counts as ignoring it.

Ignoring a notice (rejecting a real one, closing it, or leaving it open for 7 days) brings a second, angrier notice from the same sender 3 to 7 days later, and ignoring that one brings a legal request.

**Legal requests.** Government agencies send requests through the inbox with a verified mark next to the sender, and each has a deadline. Answer in time and the matter closes. Miss the deadline and the agency seizes the physical server, with every disk in it. Customers on that server lose their data unless you have a backup on another system, and the server comes back after 3 months to 2 years. Agencies mostly come after malicious customers, so who you accept matters.

A legal request shows a verified mark next to the agency's name and a countdown to its deadline, 5 to 14 days. Comply hands over what the agency asks for and costs the customer's goodwill; object keeps the customer happy, but a quarter of objections (half for shady customers) come back as a court order with a 3-day deadline. Closing a request does not answer it. When the deadline passes, the agency takes the server that runs the target customer's service: it leaves its rack, its cables and its cluster. Each workload on it restarts on another node of its cluster if one has room, from a backup if there is one and empty otherwise. Without a backup the customer's data is gone and they ask for a refund; with one they lose an outage's worth of goodwill. The server returns to storage at its location 3 months to 2 years later.

**Incidents.** The Support sidebar lists every incident under Reports: grid outages, generator failures and seizures, each with its start, how long it lasted, how much of it reached the servers, the devices and the customers it hit. Customers hit by an outage of an hour or more may ask for a refund, and some write a review.

**Shady customers.** Now and then a sales request sounds off: high volume, no questions asked, and payment in crypto. You can accept it. The crypto arrives as ordinary money in your bank account, the customer pays well and on time, and abuse reports and legal requests follow.

**Business time.** Customers expect answers and uptime on their own clock. Most keep business hours (Monday to Friday, 9:00 to 17:00, when staff work too): a ticket written Friday afternoon with a reply time of one business day is due Monday afternoon, and nights and weekends do not count against you. An outage hurts only once the customer notices it, and that depends on who is looking at that hour (economy/README.md, Noticing outages). A private customer may take a few days or three weeks, a few notice within hours; gamers notice at once in the evening and on weekends but nobody plays at 4:00 on a Tuesday, so night maintenance on game servers goes unseen; offices notice at once during business hours. An online shop or an enterprise and wants a reply within 4 hours around the clock, which takes AI bots or night staff. The Support inbox shows when each ticket is due ("15:00", "Mon 13:00", "Now"), and the customer card and each sales request show the reply time and how soon outages are noticed. Values per type are in economy/README.md, Happiness and churn.

**The cascade.** Customer happiness drops while tickets wait, and unhappy customers cancel or don't renew. Cancellations are tickets too, so a bad week snowballs into a flood. An outage creates tickets from every affected customer, weighted by segment: a flower shop writes once, an online shop three times.

**Automating support.** Support agents work through the queue at their own speed. Later, an AI support bot handles simple ticket types such as how-to and billing questions, and passes the rest to humans. A public status page (an unlock) cuts outage tickets.

**Assigning tickets.** The Support ticket header lists the support agents as chips. A ticket assigned to an agent waits for that agent, who answers it before the rest of the queue; the other agents leave it alone. Choosing Nobody hands it back, and a ticket whose agent has left the company counts as unassigned.

**Customer credit and suspension.** The right-click menu of a customer page has Credit, which takes an amount and lowers the customer's next invoice by it (what the invoice is too small to take stays for the one after), and Suspend or Unsuspend, which holds every service of the customer offline. The first suspension costs happiness and brings a ticket, as an invoice suspension does. The two Cancel subscription buttons for bots and Pear Store subscriptions ask for confirmation first.

**Replies.** Everything you or an employee do on a ticket or order goes out as an email in the thread, written in the company's voice for that situation: a template answer, a credit or refund with its amount, a retention offer, a cancellation confirmation, an unsuspension with its payment deadline, a forwarded or rejected notice, an answer to an authority, an accepted order with what was set up, where and when, and a declined order or request with its reason in the customer's terms (the product is not offered in that city, the city is full, or no reason beyond not taking it on). Each email has three wordings, picked by the seed, so the inbox does not repeat itself. Customers may write with dry humour; the company's replies stay professional.
