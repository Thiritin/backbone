# Sales and quotes

How the market above turns into order emails and sales requests in the Support app. Code: `src/sim/30-market`.

**Orders by email.** A lead asking for a basic SLA from a profile with a low or medium budget is a standard order (`orders.ts`). It names its own resources (Custom orders, below), its price starts at the request's market reference less any campaign discount, and it has the same 72 hours as a sales request. Declining it takes 10 minutes of player or support time; the steps of an accepted order take the times under Custom orders. Before a shop system every order lead writes an email. With a shop and email orders on, a quarter of the order leads (`EMAIL_SHARE`, 0.25) still write one and the rest buy a shop product at its price, and a visitor for a line without products asks for a quote instead. With Provisioning automation switched on the shop places the order at once through its module when the city has the product and room, and an order it cannot place is cancelled. Without it a shop order is accepted on arrival and waits in the inbox for its steps (Custom orders). A new game always gets its first order email within 14 days of its first panel host going online with the internet reaching it: each morning before then without an order, a friend's recommendation brings one from a private individual with chance 1 / (14 − day). Seeds 1 to 8 already brought one between day 1 and day 22 from organic leads alone, so the recommendation only fills the gap.

**Email orders off.** Switching email orders off (`website.ts`, needs an active shop) sends every order lead through the shop. Sales requests (business SLA or high budget), upgrades and the shady request are not affected. Email off therefore saves the 30 to 35 minutes of step work of a quarter of the order leads and costs only the orders the shop cannot place.

**No room on accept.** A customer who accepts a quote when no server has room signs elsewhere at once, a lost sale for lack of capacity (`sales.ts`). Upgrades work the same way.

**Custom orders.** Every order names its own resources, by email or through the shop (`spec.ts`, `manual.ts`). It starts from the size its profile's budget picks (What a lead asks for). Web hosting, VPS and game server orders scale that size by one of 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1, 1.25 or 1.5, which average to 1: web disk in half GB steps, traffic in whole GB, 1 + up to 5 × factor mailboxes, with 0.1 vCPU and 0.25 GB RAM; VPS and game server RAM in quarter GB steps, vCPUs scaled (plus one, three times in ten), disk ±25% in 5 GB steps. DNS and VPN orders scale their zones or users and their traffic the same way (DNS zones and VPN users). Other lines take the size as it is. A spec's monthly market price comes from Per-resource pricing, and the SLA factor and the profile's offer factor apply on top. Step times: quote 5, account 5, confirmation and invoice 10, domain 5, setup 15 minutes. **Quoting an email order.** The first step is the quote, at the monthly price and setup fee the player enters (the market reference and the line's setup fee are prefilled). An email order draws its budget between 1.10 and 1.25 times the reference (`ORDER_BUDGET`, the upper half of a sales lead's range, because a standard order is a shelf product), which makes a quote at the reference taken about nine times in ten, and the customer answers like a sales lead: 4 to 24 hours after the quote, 2 hours after a quote that meets their counter, taking it with the logistic chance of Customer choice, countering once at 0.97 times their accepted price when within 0.12 of it and half the time, otherwise refusing; a second refusal ends the order as a lost sale. An accepted quote has 72 hours (`REQUEST_HOURS`) for the steps. A shop order is accepted at arrival at the website's price and setup fee and has the same 72 hours. **Setup.** The setup lists the cluster and panel hosts of the order's city that sell the product, with the RAM and disk booked against what each holds; disk is the only limit (Usage curves and overcommit). Automatic (`targetFor`) and the Place orders automatically switch need the Provisioning automation skill (Know-how and unlock costs). Staff set an order up on the first host with disk room, one with the RAM for it first. The balance model counts an order as placed on acceptance, as a company with the switch on would. An order invoice is due in 14 days; an on-time payer pays it within 1 to 3 days. Upgrades ask for 1.5 or 2 times every resource.

**Domains.** Registry cost per year, 2005 to 2026, linear in between: .de €12 to €6, .at €25 to €12, .ch €15 to €9, .co.uk €8 to €7, .fr €20 to €9, .nl €10 to €6, .it €15 to €8. VeriSigh raises the .com price in steps that follow VeriSign's, each announced six months ahead: €6 in 2005, €6.42 from October 2007, €6.86 from October 2008, €7.34 from July 2010, €7.85 from January 2012, €8.39 from September 2021, €8.97 from September 2022, €9.59 from September 2023 and €10.26 from September 2024. The customer pays the year's domain price (Prices over time, Fixed-price lines) for a registration or renewal and €5 for a transfer, which includes its first year. The reminder comes 30 days before expiry.

**Registrar partners.** One contract at a time (`10-catalog/registrars.ts`, `30-customers/registrar.ts`). A domain's wholesale a year is the registry cost of the year above times the partner's factor for the tier, plus the ICANN fee on a .com (€0.18, €0.20 from July 2025). The tier is the number of domains under management (the company's and the customers') at the month start: 0, 100, 500 and 5,000. At a month start the partner invoices the signup once (first month under the contract), the fee, or for a minimum the part the domains did not use, and one twelfth of every domain's wholesale; the ledger books `ledger.registrarSignup`, `ledger.registrar` and `ledger.domains` in the category "domains". Registering and renewing book nothing; the customer pays €15 a year or €5 for a transfer, as above. Moving every domain to a new partner is a hand task of 5 minutes per domain (the Network row of the gdd By hand table). The factors follow the real reseller schemes: GoDaddy's Basic Reseller plan at USD 107.88 a year, Enom's USD 50 enrollment and the OpenSRS and Enom tiers (Essential none, Advanced USD 2,000 and 100 registrations, Premium USD 50,000 and 500, Enterprise USD 100,000 and 1,000), Name.com's Bronze, Silver and Gold at 100, 250 and 1,000 registrations, German resellers such as InterNetX with no setup fee but a monthly minimum, and Verisign's .com fee (USD 6.00 to 2007, 6.42 from October 2007, 7.85 from 2012, 8.39 in 2021, 8.97 in 2022, 9.59 in 2023, 10.26 in 2024, 10.97 from November 2026, which the .com curve above follows). The game uses the count of domains only.

**Reseller panel.** The partner's site has a reseller login once it has registered a customer domain (`30-customers/registry.ts`, `90-actions/registrar.ts`). The partner renews every domain it holds on its last day, whether or not the customer still pays, and keeps billing it monthly; a domain cancelled in the panel does not renew and is gone at expiry, and only held domains count for the bill and the tier. Moving every domain to a new partner books a year of each domain's new wholesale on arrival (`ledger.domainsMoved`) and moves each expiry a year later. A customer who leaves asks for the auth code of each domain still renewing with a chance of 0.5 the next day and waits 3 days before the wait penalty runs, even though they left. The auth code sent in the reply lets the domain go 2 days later. A refusal costs 15 happiness, and with a chance of 0.5 the customer complains to the partner, who releases the domain 2 days later anyway.

**SSL certificates.** Every certificate comes from the certificate authority VeriSigh (the VeriSign stand-in), which charges a share of the year's market price each time the customer is billed for the certificate, on its first day and at each yearly renewal: 85% for a DV, 90% for an OV and 94% for an EV certificate, so a certificate sold at the market price keeps 15%, 10% and 6%. VeriSigh invoices like the registrar partner, and before suppliers invoice it is paid straight from the cash (`CA_SHARE` in `10-catalog/products.ts`, `billingTick` in `45-billing/billing.ts`). Most customers never pay for a certificate. The share of a profile's new web hosting customers who want one at the market price is private 1%, gamer 0%, hobby developer 2%, sole proprietor 3%, small business 4%, agency 8%, reseller 3%, office 30%, online shop 60% and enterprise 80% (`CERT_WANT` in `30-customers/certificates.ts`); a customer who asks how to get the padlock wants one three times as often, at most always. While the player's shop runs, sells SSL and the website lists it, a customer who wants a certificate books it in the shop, and a rate above the market's lowers that share by market / rate. Before that, the customer opens an upgrade ticket asking for a certificate; it becomes an upgrade request for an SSL add-on to their web hosting, the player quotes a yearly price, and the customer answers like any upgrade offer. An accepted quote adds the certificate to the web hosting at the quoted yearly price. Doctor's and law offices take an OV certificate, enterprises an EV one, and online shops an EV one until browsers drop the EV address bar in 2020 and an OV one after; everyone else takes a DV one. From 2016, when certificates turn free, low-budget customers never pay for a DV certificate, and the share of medium-budget customers who still do falls by an eighth a year, from 7/8 in 2016 to none in 2023; OV and EV buyers keep paying. On 1 January of each year from 2016, each paid DV certificate stays with the share of this year's paid buyers among last year's, and the others end, so every private customer's DV certificate ends on 1 January 2016 (`30-customers/certificates.ts`).

**Company domain.** Registering it through the partner under contract costs its wholesale a month like any domain and needs no cash at once; it renews with the contract. A name is 3 to 40 letters, digits or hyphens (not at either end) in front of a TLD from the registry table above, and must not belong to a customer. Going online (the web design and listing a line on the website) is refused until it exists.

**Company email.** Per mailbox and month: every open mailbox of a paid tenant is billed at the month start after sign-up (shared mailboxes are free on Lookout 365, aliases are always free), booked in the ledger category "subscriptions" as `ledger.companyMail`. Anchors: hosted Exchange from German hosters about €10 a seat in 2005, Google Apps Premier Edition USD 50 a user a year from 2007 and Google Workspace USD 6 a user a month from 2020, Office 365 USD 6 a user a month from June 2011.

One mail server per company, on a server or in a VM. Its address is the host's public address; on a dynamic line that address is always listed (Addresses and internet), so a basement mail server works on a business line only. While the host is down or its address is listed, delivery is impaired: order emails arrive at half the rate (`IMPAIRED_SHARE` 0.5) and invoices issued in that window get their payment day 72 hours later (`MAIL_DELAY_HOURS`, applied to the first payment day and to the day a reminder sets). Without any mail service no order email arrives (`mailShare` 0). The balance model's steady bot books Hosted Exchange for the owner and every employee from the start, signs NameCheep, and pays the wholesale of the company domain and of the domains of web customers who ordered by email, against the €15 a year those customers pay; the shift moves the medians by about 0.1 to 0.2 years.

**Organic leads in the game.** Organic leads scale with the market and with awareness instead of a flat 20: 0.3 × the city's new customers × the profile's mix × its awareness × (1 + 0.5 × (level − 1)) × the review multiplier. With the starting awareness (5% for private individuals and gamers, 2% for the rest, 0.5% for web developers) Frankfurt gives about 20 leads a month in 2005, and every point of awareness a campaign buys raises organic leads too. Referrals (0.003 a month per customer with happiness 70 or more) and forum posts (2 × (0.5 + s/100) a month per employee, split over hobby developers, gamers and small businesses) come from the home city. Each forum lead also adds 0.5% of the gap to the awareness of those three profiles every month.

**VPS demand.** Smaller, cheaper VPS plans make VPS a mass market. Each profile's VPS weight is multiplied by the year's VPS demand (`VPS_DEMAND_KEYS` in `economy.ts`): 1.5 in 2005, 2 in 2011 and 4 from mid 2013, in straight lines between. The extra VPS leads come on top: a profile's organic leads, referrals and forum posts, and the cap on them, grow by its extra VPS share (`leadBoost` in `market.ts`), so web hosting and the other lines keep their lead counts. Website and campaign leads keep their count and ask for VPS at the larger share. The balance model scales each profile's VPS share the same way.

**Website and campaigns.** Their leads come from the Marketing app's lead batches. 10% of them arrive from cities without a location of yours, spread evenly; those requests arrive greyed out with "Needs a location in London", so you can see the demand you are missing.

**Lead arrivals.** Leads arrive every hour, Poisson-distributed around the monthly rate ÷ 30.4 ÷ 24, times the hour's arrival weight: the profile's attention curve from Noticing outages, scaled so a week averages 1. Small businesses, agencies, offices, online shops and enterprises write during office hours on workdays, and everyone else writes mostly in the evening. The recommended first order and the shady request still come with the 8:00 mail. Each lead draws its need first, then picks you with its share of the choice below, times its channel's conversion.

**Choice with prices.** The softmax of Customer choice decides who wins a lead, over you and the competitors that sell the product to that profile. Each host's value also carries a price term of 0.25 × λ × 100 × (1 − price factor). Your price factor is the average ratio of your running services' prices to the market reference (friends excluded, 1 without services), so undercutting wins a bigger share only gradually, as your cheaper contracts pile up. Your scores: P = 0.5 generation + 30 + 0.2 location (100 with a location in the city, 40 without); R = 0.5 uptime (60 in the first month, then from this month's down hours with a floor of 0.05% downtime) + 0.3 support (from the first replies of the last 90 days, 75% of the promised time without tickets) + 8; C = 30 + 0.3 × the share of the profile's usual products you sell.

**Requirements.** A request needs a location in its city and a cluster with nodes there (a free mounted server for dedicated). A Business or Premium SLA needs a datacenter location in the city, Premium also a company at least 12 months old, and high-budget profiles a review score of 3 stars. A request that misses one arrives greyed out and becomes a lost sale with that reason when it expires.

**Reference and budget.** The market reference is the spec's market price at the basic SLA × the SLA factor × the profile's offer (0.9, 1.0 or 1.3 by budget). Each request has a hidden budget b, uniform between 0.97 and 1.24, and the customer accepts a quote q with this probability:

```math
P(\text{accept}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{(q / \text{ref} - m) / 0.06}}, \quad m = b \cdot (1 - d) \cdot e_{\text{existing}} \cdot (1 + 0.05\,(\text{stars} - 3.5)) \cdot (1 + 0.1\,a)
```

d is the agency discount from a campaign, e is 1.15 for an existing customer and a the profile's awareness. At the reference most accept (about 85%), 20% above it about one in six. A second quote after a refusal or counter-offer has 0.35 × that chance.

**Replies.** The customer answers 4 to 24 hours after a quote. When they decline and the quote is within 0.12 of m, half of them counter at 0.97 × m × the reference; meeting the counter wins for certain. Further above they write that they have no budget. After a refusal or counter you have 48 hours for one more quote. Unanswered requests expire after 72 hours, expansion requests after 7 days.

**Winning.** An accepted quote signs the customer (payment habit drawn from Billing and payment habits) and provisions the service in the request's city at the quoted price. Without room the deal is lost as "No capacity in Frankfurt" and the customer goes elsewhere.

**Expansion.** Every upgrade ticket (2% to 4% of customers a month, see Happiness and churn) opens an expansion request for 1.5 or 2 times every resource of its service, answered in one step with the upgrade flow (`acceptUpgrade`). Winning it grows the service, in place when its server has room, and closes the ticket.

**Shady requests.** From the fourth month, about one request every three months asks for the biggest VPS, no questions asked, paid in crypto. Its budget is 1.8, so it accepts nearly any price. A won shady customer carries a hidden `shady` flag for abuse and legal follow-ups.

**Price changes.** Raising a running service's price costs happiness, lowering it wins some back, and an overpriced service keeps costing happiness every month. A raise takes effect from the month after it is made: the invoice for the current month bills the old price, and a customer who cancels over the raise leaves at that billing date without paying it. The numbers are in Happiness and churn, Price tolerance.

**Market research.** A market study reveals one profile in the Market app for good; it is a one-time payment booked as marketing. Studies cost €800 for a low-budget profile, €2,000 for a medium one and €4,000 for a high one, €20,400 for all nine (decision). The Demand section counts a city's buyers of a product as the city's new customers × each profile's mix × that profile's product weight, your part as the expected requests from the lead flows above (leads × product weight × your choice share × conversion), and splits the remainder over the competitors by their softmax among themselves. Code: `src/sim/30-market/research.ts`.

**Transfers.** From 2008, about 30% of new web hosting orders come from another host and name it in their first message. On a cPanic host the Transfer Tool copies such an account: it takes the player 15 minutes plus 5 minutes per 10 GB and runs one hour per 20 GB, at least one. A transfer that ends within 24 hours of the account's start gives the customer +3 happiness. It fails with 5%, or 15% when the host's PHP is newer than the site's app runs on; a failure opens a ticket and the player runs the transfer again.

**Message System.** The DirectAdmin Message System stores nothing. It lists the outage, how-to, abuse, upgrade and migration tickets of customers whose sites run on the host, plus a system message for each expired certificate on it and for a published security advisory that affects it. The Open button shows the ticket in Support.

**Measured flow.** `npx tsx scripts/sales-flow.ts [seed] [months]` runs the real tick with a bot that quotes 3% under the reference, answers tickets, sets up a website in month 3 and runs €250 of search ads from month 6. Seed 1 gets 6 to 9 requests a month in the basement, 16 to 26 with the website and ads, and fills the basement server at about 26 customers, after which most deals are lost for capacity until you buy hardware.
