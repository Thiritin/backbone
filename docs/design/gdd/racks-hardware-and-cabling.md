# Racks, hardware and cabling

Racks hold servers, switches and routers, and you cable them port by port. Bandwidth, redundancy and failures all follow from how you wire them.

**Racks and the shelf**

| Model | Size | Price | Where |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Shelf | 5 boards | 60 € used, the starting one free | Basement only, one |
| Rack 24U | 24U | 350 € | Basement or datacenter |
| Rack 42U | 42U | 800 € | Datacenter |
| Rack 47U | 47U | 1,000 € | Datacenter |

A new company starts with a used metal shelf in the basement, with the Fritz!Bocks standing on its top board. Each of the five boards holds one device up to 2U, a 1U or a 2U server taking one board alike, and the cables hang loose behind the boards. A shelf has no rails, so patch panels, PDUs, 0U devices and anything taller than 2U refuse it. Racks are bought later: the basement holds the shelf and at most two racks.

**Hardware**

| Device | Size | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Compute server | 1U | VPS, web and game servers |
| Storage server | 2U | Storage hosting, backups |
| GPU server | 4U | AI compute, late game |
| Access switch | 1U | Connects the servers in one rack |
| Core switch | 1–2U | Connects racks to each other |
| Edge router | 1–2U | Connects to transit and peering |
| Patch panel | 1U | Tidy cross-rack cabling; faster repairs |
| Aggregation switch | 1U | SFP fiber ports that link racks and uplinks |
| Small-office router | Shelf in a rack, 1U | The basement's internet router; its DSL port takes the line from the line jack on the carrier panel |
| UPS | 1–6U | Carries a location through a grid outage |
| Switched PDU | 0–1U | Power cycles and cuts the devices in its rack |

Switches come in several sizes: 8, 24 and 48 copper ports, each with a few SFP cages for fiber.

**Chassis and parts.** A server is a chassis with slots: DIMM slots, drive bays (3.5", 2.5" or NVMe-capable), PCIe slots and power supply bays, set by its line and generation (economy/hardware-power-and-failures.md, Hardware, power and failures). Every RAM stick, drive and CPU has a size, and a server's cores, RAM and disk are the sum of its working parts, so a failed stick lowers the RAM of its node and can push it into swapping and OOM kills when workloads book more than is left. Drives of different sizes in one array count at the smallest size. A 3.5" bay takes 3.5" drives only, a 2.5" bay takes 2.5" drives, and an NVMe drive needs an NVMe-capable bay. The Components card in the inspector lists the slots of each group (CPU sockets, DIMM slots, drive bays, PCIe slots and power supply bays) with the part in each filled slot and Add on each empty one, which offers the spare parts in the zone's storage that fit. A powered-off server can be opened from the inspector (button and right-click menu): a top-down view shows the chassis laid out by its line, the lid slides off in 600 ms, and every slot is clickable, showing the part with Replace and Remove or offering the spare parts that fit. Close puts the lid back, and the server cannot be powered on while the lid is off.

**Buying servers.** Each server in the Shop is sold as a preset, a bare chassis, or a preset with extras. A preset ships with the CPUs, RAM and drives of its model. A bare chassis keeps the board, fans, network card and one power supply and ships without CPUs, RAM and drives; it costs the preset price minus those parts at the year's part prices, and never less than a quarter of the preset price. Extras are parts added to each unit in the cart, on a preset or on a bare chassis, and each costs the year's part price for its size. Extras that do not fit the chassis (more sticks than DIMM slots, more drives than bays, a drive that does not fit the bays, more memory than the board takes) are refused, and the cart line shows the parts and the total. A preset costs its chassis plus its parts at the year's prices, so it gets cheaper as parts fall and jumps when memory and flash spike; a custom build of the same parts costs as much or more. The Parts tab sells the parts alone into storage: RAM sticks, CPUs and drives of the current generation's sizes and the generation before, drives by form factor, and power supplies, fans, network cards and mainboards. Part prices follow the years (economy/README.md, Part prices by year), and a spare part sells at the current part price.

**CPU vendors.** CPUs and mainboards come from Indel or AMB (x86, from 2005) or Ampear (ARM, from 2020), with cores per socket, speed per core, watts and price per generation (economy/README.md, CPU vendors). The lead swaps over a long game: AMB is ahead in 2005, Indel leads per core and per watt from 2008 to 2016, and from 2017 AMB brings far more cores per socket for less money while Indel keeps the faster core. Ampear cores are the slowest but draw half the watts. A server card in the Shop offers its model as Indel or AMB on a segmented control (the ARM line is Ampear only), for the preset and the bare chassis alike, and the board takes that vendor's socket: a CPU fits only a board of its vendor and the chassis's generation, so switching vendors means another chassis, or another board in a chassis without CPUs. Every socket of a server holds the same CPU. A CPU change on a multi-socket server replaces every socket in one hand task, priced for all its CPUs with one remote hands job and one downtime, and needs one spare per socket; a failed CPU replaced by the same part swaps alone. Installing a CPU into an empty socket fills every free socket with the matching spares in storage. The Parts tab sells CPUs and mainboards per vendor, and the Shop filter and the used market have a Vendor chip. Saves from before vendors give every x86 server Indel parts and every ARM server Ampear parts, and a server with two different CPUs gets the larger one in every socket.

**Brands.** Servers and switches come from parody brands whose fronts look like the real ones: Dwell (hex-mesh bezel), Hewlett-Packet (silver front with a pull tab and a perforated grille), SuperMacro (black chassis with silver drive trays) and Sysko for switches. A player can tell a rack's contents apart at a glance.

**Rack front view.** Clicking a rack shows its front: every device drawn at its real height, with status lights. Clicking a device opens it in the inspector on the right.

**Ports and cables.** Clicking a switch or router lists its ports in the inspector. For each port you pick the other end: a device in the same rack, or another rack and device from a list. Copper covers 1G, short direct-attach cables 10–25G inside a rack, and fiber 10–400G between racks. Cables between racks run through the trays. In the room, cabling is done by hand at the rack back, through the same connect action the inspector uses. Both ends must match in speed, and each port gets a role (server, uplink, or link to another switch).

**Patch panels.** A patch panel sits at the top of a rack, and the player decides what each jack is for: a carrier's cross-connect, a link to another rack, or a colocation customer's line. Incoming cables land on the back of the panel, and short patch cables connect the front jacks to switch ports. Re-patching a jack is quicker than pulling a new cable through the trays. A rented rack comes with the provider's own patch panel in its top unit, where uplinks, cross-connects and provider cables land ([Connectivity](connectivity.md)); the player's own patch panels remain for the player's own floor.

**Port groups.** Two to eight ports going to the same device can be bundled into a group (LACP). The group adds up their bandwidth and stays up when one cable or port fails. Both ends must be grouped, or the link falls back to a single port. Groups are set in a managed switch's web interface in the Browser (docs/design/lan.md), next to its VLANs and the configuration save; an unmanaged switch has no groups and forwards between all its ports.

**Server network cards.** A server's network ports are bonded in one of two modes, set in the inspector:

| Mode | How it works | Bandwidth | Switch side |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Failover | One port carries the traffic and the other takes over when it fails | One port | Nothing to set up; works across two switches |
| Load balance | All ports carry traffic (LACP) | All ports added up | Needs a port group; across two switches the pair must be stacked (an unlock) |

Failover is the safe default. Load balance doubles a server's bandwidth, but if the switch ports aren't grouped it falls back to one port and the inspector shows a warning.

**Port inspector.** Selecting a port shows the whole path as a diagram, from the switch port through any patch panel to the device on the other end, with the cable type and speed on each hop. Below it, a graph shows the port's traffic in and out over the last 24 hours.

**Technician PC.** A laptop that you plug into a free switch port gives you debug tools: ping, traceroute, a cable test that finds broken pairs, and port status. Finding the fault before sending a technician shortens the repair, because the technician brings the right part.

**Bandwidth.** Every port, switch and router has a capacity, and traffic can't exceed the slowest link on its path. One server on a 1G port can't serve 200 Gbps. Scaling up means faster ports, switches, uplinks and transit.

**Failures and redundancy.** Disks, power supplies, switches and routers fail at random, and more often as they age. Without a second path, everything behind a failed device goes offline. Two switches, two routers and servers wired to both keep services up. Until you hire technicians, you fix failures yourself.

**Lifecycle.** Every model leaves the Shop in its end of sale year: servers two generations after their own, switches, firewalls and edge routers six years after launch, UPS units eight years, and patch panels never. The Shop card shows "End of sale" with the year, the device page shows "End of support" with the year, and News reports each non-server model that goes. Devices keep working past both dates. Once a device is out of support, swapping, installing and removing parts takes twice as long, spare parts for it cost 1.5 times as much, and its components fail 1.2 times as often. While a model is on sale its price falls 5% a year from the second year, except servers, whose prices follow their parts. The newest model of each line (switches, firewalls, Sysko edge routers, the AVN, Sysko, MicroTick and Junipurr router lines, UPS units and PDUs) never leaves the Shop until a successor exists, and the 800G spine switch, the 400G firewall and the Sysko edge router XXL, all from 2027, keep the Shop stocked to the end of the game. KVMs, console servers and OOB switches never leave the Shop. See economy/hardware-power-and-failures.md, Hardware, power and failures.

**Multi-node chassis.** The Twin 2U line (from 2008, by SuperMacro and Dwell) puts four servers in 2U. One purchase brings a chassis with two shared power supplies and the fan wall, and four nodes, each with its own CPUs, RAM, drives and network card. The chassis mounts, slides, unmounts, ships and sells as one 2U unit with its nodes; a node never leaves its chassis. The rack front draws the nodes as four bays of the chassis, and the back shows each node's network ports beside the shared supplies. When both supplies fail, the chassis is switched off or a fan of the shared wall fails, all four nodes go down together; a failure inside one node takes only that node down. Each node is a server like any other: it joins a cluster, rents out as a dedicated server and has its own management controller where its brand builds one. The used market lists Twin chassis as lots of one chassis with its four nodes.

**Used hardware.** The Shop's Used market, picked at the top of the Shop and filtered by the same manufacturers and classes, sells second-hand gear from three channels, picked with a segmented control: eBuy (auctions), the WebHostingTalc marketplace (fixed asks from a hosting community board) and Craigslost deals (fixed asks in a chat group). Each month every channel posts a few listings of models that have been on sale for at least two years, spread over the month. Gear on sale at game start counts as three years older, so the market has listings from the first day; each listing shows the model, its age, a visible defect if it has one ("PSU 2 missing", "1 drive reports errors", "no rails"), the price or current bid, the time left and the shipping time. The true condition is hidden: every part arrives with its own age, so worn parts fail sooner through the normal ageing, and a drive that reports errors is already past its life expectancy. Used gear is paid in cash only and cannot be leased. On eBuy the player bids a maximum; a rival bidder has a maximum of their own, the higher maximum wins when the auction ends at its set hour, and the winner pays the other's maximum. A Craigslost seller sometimes takes the money and vanishes, which a notification reports. Board and chat sellers in the city of the destination ship in a day. The player can also list a stored device for sale with "List for sale" in the Locations storage: on the board at an ask they set, or on eBuy from half its value. A buyer comes after a while unless the ask is far above the value, and an unsold device goes back to storage when the listing expires. Fair trades on the board (a purchase or a sale there) build a reputation: from 5 the player sees new board listings a day before everyone else, and from 15 a board contact asks for a VPS once a month through the normal sales requests. Once a year a hoster near the player goes bust and sells its servers on the board as one lot, all or nothing, and once a year a datacenter clearance weekend puts a batch of auctions on eBuy; both are in the News app. Bots never buy used.

**Burn-in.** A server in a rack that is on, has power and sits in no cluster can be burned in from the inspector (Quick burn-in and Full burn-in) or the right-click menu (Burn-in). The server is busy for the test, so no customer can be put on it, and it draws full power; in a datacenter it uses its rack position and power as usual, and the test needs no remote hands. A Quick burn-in takes 6 hours and marks every part that is near its end of life as Worn in the parts list. A Full burn-in takes 48 hours, shows the real age of every part and makes about half of the parts past their life fail while no customer is on the server. A used part shows "Unknown" for its age and "Untested" as its state until a burn-in has looked at it. A server that passes a Full burn-in without a failure breaks down less often for a year. Switching the server off or losing power aborts the test. Buying on the board or eBuy comes with buyer protection: when a part of the unit fails within 14 days of its arrival, half of the unit's price comes back once, so a failure found by a burn-in pays too; Craigslost deals never refund.

**Routers.** Routers are a class of the Shop with four sources. AVN builds the Fritz!Bocks small-office routers (2006 to 2024) for the basement: every one stands on a board of the basement shelf or on a 1U shelf in a basement rack and carries traffic only while its DSL port is cabled to the line jack on the carrier panel. The basement starts with the flat's Fritz!Bocks 5050 in storage, which the player mounts before anything reaches the internet. A bought one installed from storage takes the mounted one's shelf, keeps its cables on the same ports, and the replaced router goes into storage. A basement rack moved to a datacenter leaves its router behind in storage. The basement's home line carries the lower of the line's upload and the router's routed speed, so the provided 100 Mbit router holds back a fast line until a better one is installed. Sysko sells business and branch routers from 2005, MicroTick cheap routers from 2010 and Junipurr carrier routers from 2010; the Sysko edge routers stay. All of them mount in a rack, and a datacenter router passes on no more than its throughput, shared among the links on it. A router either holds a full BGP table (and so can run an own AS) or takes provider addresses and a default route. Announcing an address block from a datacenter needs a router with a full table that is up at that location; the basement's routers never have one. Prices, speeds and which models hold a full table are in economy/README.md, Routers, UPS and PDUs.

**UPS sizes.** Eatin sells four UPS sizes: 1,500 VA (1U), 3,000 VA (2U), 5 kVA (3U) and 10 kVA (6U, with two feeds). Every location counts the battery of the UPS units in its racks against the load of its devices, so a bigger unit carries the location through a longer outage.

**Switched PDUs.** Eatin sells a switched PDU as a 1U unit with 8 outlets and as a 0U unit with 24 outlets that hangs on the rack's side rail. A rack takes one. While it is up, every device in the rack can be power cycled and powered off from the inspector and the right-click menu, also a server without a management controller, with the same corruption roll as a pulled plug and without a remote hands fee. Devices plug into the PDU without extra cabling. A reset still needs the server's own controller.

**Load balancers and storage arrays.** The Shop has neither on purpose. High availability exists only for cloud clusters and web clustering is a panel trait, so a load balancer device would have no effect on any customer product, and a storage array would only repeat the storage server lines.

**Routers and BGP.** A router passes on at most its throughput. In the basement that caps the home line at the router's routed speed (100 Mbit for the provided router, up to 2.5 Gbit for the newest small-office one). In a datacenter the limit is shared among the links on a router, so a 100 Mbit branch router turns a 1 Gbit transit port into 100 Mbit. Announcing an address block needs a router with a full BGP table at the location (an edge router M or larger, a Sysko 4451 or 8300, a MicroTick CCR, a Junipurr MX); a router without one still takes provider addresses and a default route.

**Upgrades**

- A server swaps in place for another of the same height, for example a faster 1U. Bigger machines, such as a 4U GPU server, need free space. Drain the server first, or its workloads go offline during the swap.
- A switch or router swaps in place only if the new one has at least as many ports as are in use, so you can't downgrade below the used port count. Cables stay on their ports.
- A server grows in place by adding parts from the zone's storage to its free slots, and shrinks by taking parts out; a removed part goes back to storage and fits another chassis. Drives in a RAID array, fans and power supplies (with another one working) go in and out with the server running, and a drive added to an array starts a rebuild. RAM, CPUs and drives outside an array need the server off. The work takes 30 minutes in the basement, and at a datacenter the provider's remote hands do it at their hourly rate and response time, and a technician can do it instead. The server stays off afterwards until it is powered on and boots. A server in storage takes parts at once. The mainboard and network card cannot be removed, a failed or worn part is scrapped on removal, a part replaces a component of the same kind at the same size or larger, and a drive leaves an array only while the array still has the drives its RAID level needs.
- A second power supply is a part like any other. Servers sold in a single and a dual build become the dual build once the second supply is in and the single build again when it is out.

**Server models.** Every server is described by the same handful of specs, so comparing two is quick:

| Spec | Range | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Height | 1U, 2U, 4U | Rack space |
| Architecture | x86; ARM from 2020 | ARM is cheaper per core and per watt, but a cluster runs one architecture |
| CPU | 1 or 2 sockets × cores per socket | Cores available to workloads |
| RAM | Fixed slots, filled at purchase or later | Memory available to workloads |
| Storage | Drive bays × disk type (HDD, SSD, NVMe) | Disk space and speed |
| Network | Ports × speed (1G up to 100G by year) | Bandwidth, and whether failover is possible |
| Power | Draw at idle and at full load | The power bill and the fuse limit per rack |
| Power supplies | 1 or 2 | Two connect to an A and a B feed and survive a failed supply |

The Shop app sells pre-built hardware from several manufacturers, each with its own parody logo. Within a class the manufacturers differ a little in price and failure rate, never in specs.

| Class | Lines | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Servers | Balanced 1U, compute-dense 1U, memory-optimized 2U, storage 2U, backup storage 4U, archive storage 4U, GPU 4U from 2011, ARM 1U from 2020 | One model per line and generation, so capacity can be matched to what customers need |
| Switches | Layer 2 or layer 3, with copper or SFP ports | All managed; layer 3 can route between networks and act as a gateway in later milestones |
| Security appliances | 1U firewalls with a throughput rating | Sit inline between the gateway and the switches; needed for firewall and DDoS features later |
| Patch panels | 24 or 48 ports, copper or fiber | Passive |

Storage servers hold fast disks for block and object storage. Backup servers hold many large HDDs for backups. Archive servers hold the densest, slowest drives and are the cheapest per TB, which makes archival backups a storage class customers can buy. Every model is pre-built with fixed CPU, RAM, disks, NICs and power supplies, so the choice is which model, architecture and size to buy. Bigger and denser models draw more power. Each model shows as a small isometric picture, so the sizes are easy to compare.

**Cart and leasing.** You add models to a cart and see the total before you check out. At checkout you pay in cash or lease the whole cart. A lease spreads the cost over 12, 24 or 36 monthly payments, and three leasing companies each make an offer. Offers vary at random from good to bad and change every month, so it pays to compare them. Leased hardware works like bought hardware, but it can't be sold until it is paid off. Buying it out early costs the remaining payments, and after the last payment it is yours.

**Storage.** Each availability zone has its own storage room. At checkout you choose the zone the Shop delivers to. An order is in transit for 3 days in 2005, falling to 1 day by 2015, and cannot be mounted until it arrives; the Cart shows the wait, and the first orders of a company without a server arrive at once. Units are mounted from the rack view of that zone by clicking a free slot, or from the storage list with Mount, which asks for the rack as cards and then the slot on that rack's front. Unmounting puts them back into the same zone's storage and drops their cables. At home the founder racks a unit at once; at a datacenter racking and unracking is hand work like a repair, done by remote hands for a fee, by you on site, by a friend or by a technician at the location, and the unit shows the work under way and stays off until it is done. Sending stored units to another zone is a shipment that costs and takes as long as a rack move. Identical units in storage form a stack, and a stack can be mounted in one step, each unit directly on top of the last. Hardware sells from storage or from a rack at its resale value: 90% of the price right after buying, 80% after the first month, falling evenly to 20% over the next two years. A board or eBuy buyer never pays more than that value, and a server stripped of a part loses that part's price, so stripping and reselling never beats selling the whole server. A new generation arrives every three years, and each one packs more resources per U and per watt, so replacing old servers cuts the energy bill as well as freeing space.

**Copy and paste.** A finished rack can be saved as a template and pasted into an empty rack slot. The game orders the hardware, and technicians install and cable it. A typical large location ends up with one core routing rack and many server racks, each with its own pair of switches.

**Rack view.** The left side shows the rack name, a Front and Back switch and the rack drawn large, every unit on its own with its number, so free units are as visible as used ones. Rack facts, power draw, utilization and upgrades (the basement's extra circuit, a rented rack's feed B and high-density feed) sit in cards on the right. On the back, cables leave each port to the right, run in a cable channel beside the rack, one lane per cable, and come back left into the other port. Devices outside the rack that it is or can be cabled to (the carrier panel, patch panels, a switch in another rack) sit above it as small labelled boxes, and their cables climb the channel into them. You drag from one port to another on the rack back to lay a cable.

**Port numbers and lights.** Every port face prints its numbers like real hardware: a switch numbers odd ports above the top row and even ports below the bottom row, a server counts its NICs from 0 as eth0, a patch panel counts each row from 1 and the carrier panel counts from 1. Each jack and SFP cage has a link light. It is dark without a link (nothing plugged in, a free patch jack behind the cable, or either end down), steady green with a link, and amber when the link runs slower than the port, such as a 10G cage cabled to a 1G port. With traffic it flickers, faster the larger the share of the link in use, on a logarithmic scale so a hobby site flickers slowly and a full link about five times a second. Each server's traffic follows its shortest cable path to the carrier panel and lights every port on the way. Switches and firewalls repeat the lights on their power-supply side as a numbered panel, one light per port. Patch panels are passive and have no lights. With reduced motion, the lights stay steady.

**Cabling manager.** Each location has a cabling view that lays every port of the location out on one plane, in three columns: the carrier panel, the network (switches, firewalls and patch panels) and the servers grouped by rack. Each device lists its ports top to bottom, and every cable runs from the edge of one port row to the other. You drag from one port to another to lay a cable, and the game picks copper, DAC or fiber from the port types and whether both ends are in the same rack. Ports that can't connect dim while you drag. Clicking a cable selects it so you can remove it. The rack view and the cabling view show the same cables.

**Wear, failures and maintenance.** Every device is built from components that wear out: drives, power supplies, fans, RAM, the mainboard, the CPU and network cards in servers, and power supplies, fans and the board in switches and routers. Each component type has a life expectancy. Before it, failures are rare; after it, they become likely, and drives fail far more often than RAM or mainboards. A failed component shows as an amber or red light on the device front, and what happens depends on what broke:

- **Drive:** in a RAID array the server keeps running degraded until the drive is replaced and rebuilt; without RAID the data on it is lost and the server goes down.
- **Power supply:** with two, the server keeps running on the other; with one, it goes down.
- **Fan:** the other fans spin up and the device runs 6 degrees hotter until the fan is replaced, which can push it past the heat thresholds below.

**Heat.** Every location has a room temperature, shown next to its power in the Locations view; a server's management page shows its own inlet. A datacenter hall holds 22 degrees and the basement follows the seasons from 17 to 27. When a hall's cooling fails, or the basement's servers draw more than its walls shed, the room heats up, and the more kW each position draws, the faster it climbs: a full rack passes 35 degrees within minutes. From 35 degrees servers throttle and the customer apps on them run slower. At 45 they shut themselves off, which is an outage like a power cut, and they boot again once the room is back under 30. A server with failed fans runs its CPU hotter: from 85 degrees CPU it throttles, down to half its speed at 95, which its management event log records. Every hour a server spends at 35 degrees inlet or 90 degrees CPU or more wears its components, which fail more often from then on. Numbers in economy/README.md, Cooling and overheating.
- **RAM, mainboard, CPU or network card:** the device goes down until the part is replaced.

Spare parts are bought in the Parts tab of the Shop and kept in the zone's storage. You replace a part from the device in the Locations view. Drives in a RAID array, fans and one of two power supplies can be swapped while the device runs; everything else needs the device powered off, so its workloads go offline unless you migrate them first. At a datacenter the provider's remote hands do the swap, at their hourly rate and response time. A technician at the location can do it instead (see Staff). A spare replaces a component of the same kind at the same size or larger, and a drive spare has to fit the bays of the chassis. Replacing worn parts before they fail is your call, and it is cheaper than an outage.

**Hand work at a datacenter.** Every hand task (mounting or unmounting a device or mounting a whole stack, replacing, installing or removing a part, pulling the power, pressing the server's front buttons or plugging its cords, resetting a management controller) offers four ways in one sheet, each with its time and cost: Remote hands, Technician, Do it yourself and Send a friend. Do it yourself starts the work after the drive from your home city: 1 hour in the same city, 4 hours in the same country, 9 hours in another European country, and it is not offered overseas. The drive costs a fare and keeps you busy for the drive there, the work and the drive back, so the replies in your Email queue wait. The premium room of a city, the one with the highest position price, only lets visitors in on a booked slot, which adds a day. Send a friend costs a round of pizza and beer, can be done 3 times a year, leaves you free and has one chance in ten of a mishap that doubles the time the work takes. Servers set up at home can go to a datacenter in your own car, and you rack them yourself when you arrive.

**RAID.** Each server's drives run in a RAID level you choose in the BIOS setup on the server's screen (see Server console), or on the server page while it is off: none, RAID 1, RAID 5, RAID 6 or RAID 10. A new level deletes the array and asks first when it holds data. More redundancy means less usable space and more drive failures survived. A rebuild runs at the rebuild rate you set in the setup or, on a server with a controller, in its Storage tab: a fast rebuild slows the server, a slow one leaves it exposed for longer. Distributed storage pools across servers, Ceph style, are a later unlock.

**Power and labels.** Any device can be powered off and on from the Locations view; everything that depends on it goes down with it. A device that is switched on boots for one hour before it carries traffic. Every port sits on the back of the rack, so cables are drawn in the back view. Clicking a power supply's inlet on the rack plugs it in or pulls the plug, asking first when that takes devices offline. Switches and firewalls mount reversed, as in real racks: their port face looks to the rack back, next to the server NICs, and their power supplies and fans look to the rack front, so a switch is plugged in from the front. Servers show drive caddies, a control panel and status lights on the front, and NICs and power supplies on the back. Device names are small asset tags that start with the zone, such as fra1-srv-02, from the moment a unit lands in storage; a unit mounted in another zone takes that zone's next name. A server, a rack and a location each show CPU, memory, storage and network use as a bar of 20 lights: green, then 4 orange, then 2 red. Devices can be given a custom label, and cables a custom colour.

**Remote management.** Servers from the 2008 generation (Dwell and Hewlett-Packet) or the 2011 generation (SuperMacro and Lenovoid), and every ARM server, carry a management controller: iDRAQ on Dwell, iLOL on Hewlett-Packet, IPMI on SuperMacro and XClarify on Lenovoid. The 2005 generation has none. A controller has its own address on a management network (192.168.3.x in the basement, 10.n.2.x in a datacenter), shown on the server page with a link that opens the Browser. Its page imitates the brand's real one, logs in with one click, and offers Power (power on, graceful shutdown, reset, power cycle), Sensors (inlet temperature of the room, fan speeds with a failed fan at 0 RPM, power supply status) and the Event log of the server's last 20 events. It keeps answering while the server is off. The Locations inspector and the right-click menu route a server's power through the controller and say "via iDRAQ"; a server without one offers Shut down while its operating system answers, and Pull power on site. Pulling power or pressing the front buttons on site is instant in the basement and while you are at the datacenter; from afar it costs remote hands for an hour, after their response time, or a friend's drive.

**Server console.** Every server has a screen, reached three ways: a Console tile on the server page and a Remote console button on the controller's page open it in its own window (iDRAQ Virtual Console, iLOL Remote Console, SuperMacro iKVM, XClarify Remote Control), a KVM on the console end opens the same window from its page with the power keys the KVM can press, and clicking the blue console end on the back of a server in the rack plugs in a monitor, which replaces the server page with the screen and the buttons at the machine while the player stands at that location. The screen shows "No signal" while the server is off, the POST splash while the server boots (three game minutes, so pausing the game is the sure way to catch it), the firmware prompt a stuck boot waits at, the BIOS setup, and then the operating system's own first screen (a Debian login, VMwhere's direct console, a Windows logon) or "Operating system not found". Del or F2 during the splash or at the firmware prompt opens the BIOS setup; F1 at the prompt continues the boot. A server in setup never finishes booting, so its workloads stay down and its status reads "In BIOS setup" until F10 or Esc leaves it; a reset or a pulled plug leaves it too. The setup's Storage page holds the RAID level and rebuild rate. F11 during the splash shows the boot menu after it (hard disk, USB, network); the USB entry boots the installer on the stick, which is always plugged in and holds both operating systems. The installer lists Debiant GNU/Lunix and Windoors Server with the version of the year and their install hours, and offers Erase disk and Reboot. Picking one shows the target: the array as the setup configured it and what the disks hold. Empty disks install at once; software nobody uses is erased first; software with customers on it lists what dies and needs the server's hostname typed, then loses the data exactly as a dead array does (the loss report, the refund tickets, the reputation hit) and ends those services. The OS installs over its install hours (one for Lunix, two for Windoors); a reset, a pulled plug or a shutdown aborts it and leaves the disk empty, and the finished install boots the server from its disk to its login. Every boot of an installed machine adds the OS's own boot minutes to POST. Keys come from the keyboard while the screen has focus and from a row of soft keys under it.

**Hangs and pulled plugs.** A server's operating system can hang: its workloads are down and it answers nothing, so a graceful shutdown is refused. Reset and Power cycle through the controller clear it, as do Pull power and a technician on site. A hung controller refuses every action and its page does not load; a hand task of 15 minutes on site (remote hands in a datacenter) resets it, and a hung server with a hung controller leaves only Pull power. Any hard stop of a server that ran workloads (pulled power, reset, power cycle) risks corrupting them: when the array has its redundancy intact the server only resyncs, slowed like a rebuild; otherwise each corrupted workload is down until it is recovered, which takes 30 minutes of hand work (in the Locations inspector, through a technician, and in the Terminal later). Most workloads come back fine; the rest need a restore from backup and are lost without one. A graceful shutdown never corrupts anything.

**Remote access.** A server without a controller can still be reached from afar through a KVM, and gear through a console server. Every server has a console end on its back, and switches and rack routers have a console port and a management port beside their data ports, drawn on the back view. These cables carry no customer traffic and count only for remote access.

- The PiKVN (Pear Street Electronics, 2019, 60 €) hangs on the rack rail without a rack unit. It takes a switch port for its own network and one cable to a server's console end, and switches the server's power through its own ATX adapter.
- The IP-KVN 8 (Avoscent, 2005, 1U, 1,200 €) serves eight servers but only shows their consoles. An ATX adapter (25 €, fitted from the server page) lets it switch that server's power as well.
- A server whose KVM can switch its power counts as remotely switchable: the inspector routes power through it and says "via PiKVN".
- The OpenGearbox 8, 16 and 48 console servers (2005, 1U, 1,500 €, 2,500 € and 6,000 €) connect to console ports with rollover cables.
- The OOB switch 8 (Rarytan, 2005, 1U, 150 €) links management ports and console servers into a network of their own.
- A KVM or console server is reachable when its network port sits on a working switch with a way out, or on an OOB switch that has its own way in. A console server or OOB switch can get its own way in through a management DSL line at 20 € a month or, from 2008, a cellular modem at 30 € a month. That path stays up while the production network is down.
- Each KVM and console server has an address on the management network (192.168.4.x in the basement, 10.n.3.x in a datacenter) and a plain page in the Browser. The page lists every device behind it with its power state, the last lines of its console, and buttons for power, Continue boot and Recover over console.
- The Terminal's `console <device>` command opens the console of a server, switch or router over the same path.
- KVMs fail at 4% a year. None of these devices needs research; the year gates them.

**Stuck boots.** After a part swap or a hard power event (lost power, Reset, Power cycle), a server has a 5% chance to stop at a BIOS prompt and wait for a key. Its workloads stay down until someone presses it: Continue boot takes 2 minutes over a controller or a console, and is a hand task on site otherwise.

**Power plugs.** Every power supply has to be plugged in before the device gets power. A newly mounted device arrives with its supplies unplugged; the Power card in the inspector lists each supply with Connect and Disconnect, and the rack back draws each plugged supply's cord running left behind the rack rail to the power strip (a Schuko strip in the basement, the PDU at a datacenter). A device runs while at least one plugged supply works: with two supplies plugged in, one can fail and the device keeps running, while a single plugged supply that fails takes it down. A switched-on device boots for one hour once it gets its first working supply. Unplugged devices draw nothing. The starting hardware comes plugged in.

**Power feeds.** A rented position comes with one power feed at the datacenter's kW limit. You can add a second feed (B) so that servers with two power supplies survive a feed failure, or a high-density feed that raises the kW limit. The basement can get one extra household circuit. A circuit carries 80% of its limit continuously: a room that draws more than its limit for an hour trips its main breaker and goes dark, and a feed that stays above 80% for three hours trips as well. The player resets a tripped breaker in the basement or an office by hand in the fuse box once the load fits again; at a datacenter the provider resets it for the remote hands fee. Servers with two power supplies split their draw over A and B; everything else hangs on feed A. When one feed fails or trips, the dual-fed servers move their whole load to the other feed, and if that is more than one feed's limit, the other feed trips too and the whole rack goes dark. A rack is only redundant while A and B together stay under one feed's limit. The rack view shows the draw of each feed against its limit with the 80% mark, and raises an alarm for a feed above 80% and for a rack whose two feeds together draw more than one feed can carry.

**Monitoring.** Without monitoring, the only way to notice a failure is to see the lights in the Locations view, or to wait for customers to complain. Monitoring software is an early research unlock. It notifies you of every failure and warns about parts past their life expectancy. It also opens the Monitoring app, a dashboard of every location, rack, feed, link and server with live usage and a list of alerts. Like other software, it comes in a few products with different prices.

**Keeping it fun at scale.** Cabling port by port is fun for the first few racks and tedious at 200. Three things fix that: rack templates, an auto-cable button that wires a server to the next free ports on its rack's switches, and technicians who do installs for you.

**Device inspector.** A selected device shows its front or back, then its status as plain values: power (On, Off, Booting, Unplugged, No power), link, and for a server its control plane and role, with Set up while it has none. One row of four meters shows CPU, memory, storage and network, followed by the ports, the cluster node, the power supplies, components and specifications as flat sections. Power on and off sit in the header and in the device's menu. A Balanced 1U with one power supply shows its empty second bay with an Add button and the price of the second supply.
