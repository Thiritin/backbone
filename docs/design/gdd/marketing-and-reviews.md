# Marketing and reviews

Marketing brings in new customers, and reviews decide how many of them trust you. You can run basic campaigns yourself; review platforms need a marketing manager.

**Your own channels.** Besides paid campaigns, the Marketing app manages the acquisition channels you own and grow step by step. You start with email only: customers find you through friends and write to your inbox, and every order is a conversation. Every company has a basic website from the start (see The company website); next you buy a web design, which makes the site findable and brings in a steady trickle of requests. The website then gets extended with a shop system, so customers can order standard products on their own and the order lands as a ready service instead of an email. The web design and the shop can also be set up from day one (the Marketing app sets up channels), so a first-year company can switch on automatic orders before it learns Marketing campaigns. With a website in place you can run Google ads (search ads below) that send visitors to it, and later AI ads, which target better and cost more. Each step is a one-off setup cost plus upkeep, and the website and shop are also where your prices and reviews show.

**Staff in the community.** Once you have employees, you can assign one to post in hosting and gaming forums. Helpful posts bring in customers slowly and cheaply, and the effect grows with the employee's skill. Bigger companies can send staff to trade fairs and industry meetups. Fairs bring business customers, and the people you meet there get you better transit prices and make networks more willing to peer with you.

Five fairs run every year: "SeeBIT" (CeBIT, until 2018), "CloudFeast" (CloudFest), the "RIPE-ish Meeting" (RIPE Meeting), the "DE-SIX Summit" (DE-CIX Summit) and "Data Centre Whirled" (Data Centre World). Each has a date, a cost, a required company level and an audience. Booking one sends an employee of any role, who does no other work for the fair's days. When the fair ends the attendee brings back leads from the fair's business audiences, which arrive over the following month, plus awareness with those audiences and network contacts. The contacts add the fair's bonus to your goodwill with networks, which lowers transit prices and makes peering requests more likely, and fades month by month. A marketing manager trained on fairs brings back half again as many leads. If the attendee has left the company by then, the stand stays empty and the money is gone.

**Campaigns.** In the Marketing app you pick a channel, the profiles to target and a monthly budget.

| Channel | Reaches | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Word of mouth | Friends, gamers | Free and slow; the only channel in the basement |
| Search ads ("Goggle Ads") | Anyone searching for hosting | Paid per click; needs a website |
| AI ads | Anyone, with better targeting | Unlocks late; costs more per click and converts better; needs a website |
| Gaming communities | Gamers | Sponsor a streamer or a server list |
| Trade fairs | Businesses, enterprises, carriers | Expensive; needs staff; only pays off at higher company levels; improves transit prices and peering acceptance |
| Forum posts | Hobbyists, gamers, small businesses | Needs an employee assigned; cheap and slow |
| Cold calling | Small shops, local businesses | Cheap to start but converts poorly; the cost per customer depends on who you call. Reaches businesses that never search for hosting |
| Agency outreach | Web agencies | Targets agencies with a discounted offer; they bring their clients' later VPS needs |
| Partner program | Agencies, small businesses | Commission per referred customer |

**Reviews.** Customers leave reviews after tickets, outages and cancellations. By default you can only read them and use the free listing. ProvenExpat and TrustPylon need a marketing manager on staff; when the last one leaves, the company falls back to the free listing at the next month start:

| Platform | Cost | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Free listing | Free | Collects reviews; the trust boost comes from the HOSTtoast listing |
| "ProvenExpat" (ProvenExpert) | Moderate monthly fee | Review invites, a website badge; medium boost |
| "TrustPylon" (Trustpilot) | High monthly fee | Strong marketing tools; the biggest boost |

The review score feeds into your reputation and into the host requirements customers check.

**How the Marketing app works.** Marketing is preinstalled and free. Until the Marketing campaigns skill is learned it shows only Domain, where the company buys its own domain: a registrar picker while no contract exists, a name field with the suggested name and a Buy button that signs the contract and registers the domain together; once bought it shows the domain, registry, registrar and yearly price. The skill opens the other sections. Channels lists the owned steps as cards: email is always on, the web design comes first, and the shop, Goggle Ads and AI ads each need the web design. AI ads become available in 2023. Campaigns shows one card per paid channel (Goggle Ads, AI ads, gaming communities, cold calling, agency outreach) with the audiences it reaches as toggles and a monthly budget as a segmented control; agency outreach also offers a discount of 10%, 20% or 30%. A campaign's budget is billed at each month start, and so are website and shop upkeep and the review platform fee. Campaigns spread their budget evenly over the cities where you have a location. Awareness shows how well each audience knows you. Reviews shows the score, the latest reviews and the platforms, of which you use one at a time; paid platforms show that they need a marketing manager until one is hired. Trade fairs shows the next run of every fair as a card with its city, dates, cost, company level, leads, audience and network contacts, the employees free on those days as chips, and a Book button. Community lists the running and past sponsorships. Forum posts come with the Staff app; the partner program comes later.

**Marketing manager.** A marketing manager trains on campaigns, review platforms and trade fairs. Trained on campaigns and working the queue, the best one adds 5% to 15% to every campaign's leads by skill. Trained on review platforms, they make platform invites 50% more frequent.

**Community events.** From the first weeks, a club or meetup asks for a free service now and then: a request arrives in the inbox every 4 to 8 weeks, drawn from the events open at the company's level. Each names the service, its size and how long it should run, such as two game servers for the weekend, a VPS for three months or web hosting for a year. Accept provisions the services at no charge through the normal placement, so they take capacity, traffic and an IPv4 address like any customer, and the accept fails with the usual error when there is no room. Decline closes the request without any penalty, and a request left unanswered lapses after two weeks without one either. The services end by themselves when the event does. At the end the event pays out. A technical event (the Chaos Computing Club meetup, the Hack-a-Sorus hackathon, the Debonair Linux mirror, from level 3 the DevOops Conference) adds know-how, and with a 20% chance a recommendation puts a technician with 20 more skill than a typical applicant into the Staff market for a month. A public event (the LAN-tastic LAN party, the Frag-Fest tournament, the Linux User Group website) raises the awareness of its audience once, with a 50% chance of a 5-star review, and brings 3 to 6 leads of that audience in the month after. If a sponsored service was down for more than 2 hours during the event, none of that happens: two 1-star reviews appear and the awareness of the event's audience drops by 10%, since everyone saw the logo. Overbooked nodes make this likely. The organiser is a hidden customer who pays nothing, is not counted among customers and does not churn; their outage emails reach the inbox like any other.

**The sponsor board.** Sponsor-Bored, at sponsorbored.net in the Browser, is a site where the player posts an offer on purpose instead of waiting for a request. The site is listed in the site index under Community from the start of the game. An offer names a product (game server, VPS, storage or web hosting), one of the line's sizes, a duration of 1, 3, 6 or 12 months, 1 to 4 slots, and a price of free or 50% off. At most three offers are open at once. Over the next 2 to 10 days, two more applications than there are slots arrive in the inbox as sponsoring tickets, from a clan, a small LAN party, a fan site, a student project or an open source mirror. Each ticket names the audience size, the audience and how the applicant advertises: a banner on its site, a forum signature or a mention on stage. Accepting runs the usual sponsorship with one service for the whole term, at EUR 0 as a hidden organiser or at half the list price as a normal customer who leaves when the term ends; the accept fails with the usual error when there is no room. When every slot is taken, the remaining applications close. The player can withdraw an open offer, which closes its waiting applications and stops new ones, while sponsorships already running carry on. While a sponsorship runs it raises the awareness of the applicant's audience a little each month and brings a batch of leads of that audience, and the board adds no more awareness once it has added 0.03 in total. About one applicant in ten misuses the server: the hidden customer draws abuse and legal notices like a shady customer. About one applicant in seven drops the banner after the first month, which stops the awareness and the leads and shows a notification. Downtime of more than 2 hours turns the end of the sponsorship into two 1-star reviews and a drop of the audience's awareness, as in a community event. The site lists the open sponsoring requests from community events, the open offers, the applicants with Accept and Decline, and the running sponsorships. Each request shows the community, its audience and how many people it reaches, the services it needs and for how long, what it gives back (a mention at the event or know-how) and the day it lapses. The player answers a request with a free offer, which starts the sponsorship at once, or with 50% off, which the community takes or turns down for another sponsor a day later; a request answered with 50% off waits for that answer even past its two weeks.
